Thematic Analysis with Large Language Models: does it work with
languages other than English? A targeted test in Italian
Stefano De Paoli – Abertay University (s.depaoli@abertay.ac.uk)



Abstract
This paper proposes a test to perform Thematic Analysis (TA) with Large Language Model (LLM)
on data which is in a different language than English. While there has been initial promising work
on using pre-trained LLMs for TA on data in English, we lack any tests on whether these models
can reasonably perform the same analysis with good quality in other language. In this paper a test
will be proposed using an open access dataset of semi-structured interviews in Italian. The test
shows that a pre-trained model can perform such a TA on the data, also using prompts in Italian.
A comparative test shows the model capacity to produce themes which have a good resemblance
with those produced independently by human researchers. The main implication of this study is
that pre-trained LLMs may thus be suitable to support analysis in multilingual situations, so long
as the language is supported by the model used.



Keywords: Large Language Models, Thematic Analysis, Qualitative Interviews, Multilingualism,
Similarity



   1. Introduction
With recent prominence gained by Large Language Models such as GPT3.5. GPT4, LLama-2 or
Mistral, the social sciences community has started to explore their application to qualitative data
analysis. The use of LLMs for Thematic Analysis (TA), in particular, has shown good results, with
solid propositions (see e.g. Author, 2023; Drapal et al., 2023) attempting to replicate most of the
well know 6 phases of TA proposed by Braun and Clarke (2006) in their seminal work on this
analysis method.

Thus far the existing publications have proposed performing TA with LLMs on data in English
(e.g. semi-structured interviews in English). However, to be potentially viable as a method, the use
of LLMs for TA should be assessed also with data in languages other than English. This may be
particularly relevant for qualitative analysis, since for example qualitative interviews are often
performed in national languages, studying local communities and with actors which do not
necessarily speak English. Additionally, nuances of the language may be important for qualitative
research and analysis. Moreover, this would better support multilingualism in social research,
against strict dominance of English, or facilitate the work of researchers in the areas of language
and multilingualism.

The goal of this paper is to present and discuss the results of conducting a TA supported by LLMs
(specifically the commercially available models GPT3.5-Turbo and GPT4-Turbo), of data in
Italian language. The choice of this language for the test is, first and foremost, practical, since the
author speaks Italian and can therefore understand the nuances of the language and the data used
(see later). Nonetheless, Italian constitutes a good case test for the purpose of the paper. It is a
Romance/Latin language, thus sufficiently different from English. Moreover, it is a major
European language, and it does appear a language supported by the LLMs used for this work (in
other words the LLMs used were pre-trained also with material in Italian, albeit we can expect less
than the equivalent English training data).

For performing this test, I will adopt a process similar to the one I proposed in previous papers,
see Author (2023a, 2023b) and Author and Colleague (2024). This entails reproducing some of
the key phases of TA proposed by Braun and Clarke (2006; 2021). In particular, in this paper I
will perform the initial coding (phase 2), the definition of themes (phase 3), and a revision of the
themes (phase 4). For the analysis I selected an Open Access dataset of semi-structured interview
transcriptions by Gualandi et al. (2022) available from Zenodo, performed with arts and humanities
scholars at the University of Bologna in Italy. The focus of the authors’ research was to investigate
the relations of humanities scholars with the term data, and to understand what they mean with
‘data’. A related paper was also published by the authors (Gualandi et al., 2023). The Zenodo
deposit also contains a file reporting the qualitative analysis (performed with the Open-Source
software QualCoder) which was performed using Grounded Theory (GT). Although GT and TA
are different methodologies, they share some common elements such as a phase of initial coding,
and a second phase of identification of higher-level categories (like axial coding or identification
of themes). The assumption is that we can use the coding analysis performed by the authors for
some comparison at least. More details about the original analysis will be discussed in the
methodology section of the paper. Ultimately, as previously written, the goal of performing an
inductive TA with an LLM is not to reproduce exactly what a human analyst would do, but rather
to deliver an analysis which is ‘good enough’ in capturing key patterns in a dataset. Two human
analysts performing inductively a TA may, themselves, produce some different themes.

In the following pages the paper will present the following: first a brief review of the state-of-the-
art of using LLM for qualitative analysis; second a thorough discussion of the methods and
materials of the research work; third a presentation of the results covering initial codes and themes;
fourth, an evaluation of the results to compare the LLM themes with the original categories
produced by Gualandi et al. (2022). The paper will then present a discussion of the findings and a
conclusion.
   2. Literature review: LLM for qualitative analysis
Thematic Analysis is a widely diffused analysis procedure for qualitative textual data and other
data formats. It is often adopted because of its flexibility and because it is generally theory agnostic
and can be used within different forms of theorising (see e.g. Glasdamet al., 2023 or Glasdam and
Stjernswärd, 2022 for two example using Goffman or Weber, and Aitken et al. 2019 using
Bourdieu) and in a variety of social sciences disciplines and investigation areas. Its application
includes research in e.g. medical and nursing research (see e.g. Vaismoradi et al., 2013 for an
influential paper), social work (see Clark et al., 2023 for a recent contribution), sociology of work
and labour (Nicholas and McDowall, 2012) or social media (Deighton-Smith and Bell, 2018) to
mention a few examples. Braun and Clarke are generally considered the key authors of the
approach and have written several influential papers which have popularised it, describing the
method (2006; 2021), showing its application (e.g. 2018) and further contributing to other
methodological aspects of the debate (2013; 2019), within the context of qualitative research. But
other influential authors had written about the approach before them including Merton (1975) or
Aronson (1994). For this paper the most important component of TA is that the approach proposed
by Braun and Clarke in their seminal 2006 paper, sees the method perform 6 inter-related phases:
1) familiarisation with the data; 2) generation of initial codes; 3) grouping and sorting of codes
into themes; 4) review of the themes; 5) summarisation and renaming of the themes; 6) write-up
of the results.

With the recent and disruptive advent of Large Language Models, researchers have started to
consider these models’ capacity to perform qualitative forms of analysis. LLMs are AI systems
working with Natural Language Processing. They are trained on a very large amount of textual
material and can produce natural language conversations (e.g. in a chatbot like chatGPT), but can
also perform other ‘completion’ tasks on textual content such as summarisation of text (see e.g.
Wang et al., 2023), or identification of topics for example. In the field of textual analysis, LLMs
have started to be used to perform certain forms of Qualitative Analysis. For example, this paper's
author proposed a methodology which reproduces the 6 phases of TA by Braun and Clarke (see
Author, 2023a, 2023b) with some success, performing the process entirely inductively. This
approach uses the commercial OpenAI GPT models via the Application Program Interface (API)
using python and passing the data (semi-structured interviews) programmatically to the LLM for
the analysis. This same approach has further been tested by Mathis et al. (2024) with medical semi-
structured interviews using another Open Source pre-trained LLM called Llama-2. Testing for this
paper has shown a good degree of quality in the analysis by performing a comparison between a
human made analysis and the LLM analysis, using semantic similarity and other techniques.
Drapal et al. (2023) also proposed an emulation of phases 2-4 of TA with LLMs, using legal
material in the form of facts descriptions from criminal court opinions as data, and also offering
an evaluation comparing human and LLM codes. Author and Colleague (2024) also proposed a
metric for measuring the saturation of initial coding (the phase 2 of the analysis), based on the ratio
between the total number of initial codes and the number of unique (non-repeated codes). Zhang
et al. (2023a) performed empirical research to assess how chatGPT can be used by researchers to
refine Thematic Analysis. This uses the chatbot, rather than accessing programmatically the API
as in the previously cited literature. Hamilton et al. (2023) also tested the use of chatGPT as support
tool for performing qualitative thematic analysis, in this case also the chatbot is used rather than
the API.

Other authors have focused only on the initial coding phase of the process, often employing a
deductive approach, where codes are decided in advance and the model is asked to then use the
codes to categorise the data. Works in this category for example include the papers by Gao e al.
(2023) and Xiao et al. (2023). These deductive works often adopt inter-coder reliability between
LLMs and human analysis to measure quality, which is possible because of the deductive nature
of the process they adopt. Zhang et al. (2023b) also designed an early prototype tool supporting
initial coding for a Thematic Analysis, even if they call these initial codes as themes. The works
just cited have often a more software engineering-oriented approach than other works, with
potentially less focus on discussing methodological issues that may arise when using LLM for
qualitative analysis. Few other studies are performing other types of textual analysis. Sentiment
analysis is, as one would expect, a quite popular approach (see for an applied example Zhang et
al., 2023, or Carneros-Prado et al., 2023 for a study also encompassing emotion detection). Chew
et al. (2023) have also proposed a process for deductive coding for content analysis with LLMs,
whilst Huber and Carenini (2022), amongst others, offered reflections on discourse analysis with
LLMs, in particular focusing on the structure of discourse.

As far as investigating the multilingual capacities (beyond the English dominance) of pre-trained
LLMs in Thematic Analysis, the previously cited work by Drapal et al. (2023) used data (court
documents) in Czech language. This appears so far, the only explicit case where data other than
English was used for TA with LLMs. However, it does seem that the material was translated in
English for performing the analysis, using automated translation. So even if the original data was
in Czech, this was translated to English for the analysis. In any case, the authors’ focus is not on
testing the multilingual TA capacities of LLMs, and no reflections are therefore offered by them
in this sense. In the field of evaluation of LLMs multilingual capacities (i.e. computing studies
related to Natural Language Processing), there have been studies which have tried to assess the
multilingual capacities of some LLMs (see for example Armengol-Estapé et al., 2021 for a study
on Catalan on the GPT models, and Chang et al, 2023 for a survey which covers also existing
literature on multilingual evaluations). These studies, however, are not focused on analysis in the
social sciences, but more on an evaluation of the performance of the computational capacities of
the models, when they deal with multilingual tasks. The following pages will propose therefore
the study of performing TA in a language other than English with a test using data and prompt in
Italian, as previously anticipated. This can be a first step toward addressing the gap of whether an
LLM supported TA can be done with material in a variety of languages.
   3. Method and Procedure
The process for performing the TA inductively with LLMs (the GPT3.5-Turbo and GPT4-Turbo
models in this research), sees the use of python scripts to interact with the LLMs API in a
programmatic fashion. In essence, python scripts are used to pass the data to an LLM, and prompts
are used to instruct the model on the operations it should do on the data. The model will then return
the response of the operation. Figure 1 summarises the process for the initial coding (phase 2 of
TA) where the interviews are passed in the prompt within a python script which sends the interview
and the instructions to the LLM API, which returns the set of initial codes formatted in JSON
objects as response. The process is the same for the identification of themes, but for this it is the
set of initial codes which is programmatically sent to the LLM, returning then a set of themes and
the underlying codes.




Figure 1 – Programmatic access of an LLM API for the Initial Coding

Key to these operations is the ‘prompt’, the set of instructions we provide to the LLM to perform
actions on the data. A prompt in a chatbot like chatGPT would look like the one in Figure 2, where
for example we ask the model to name us the 3 most important Italian poets of all time (the prompt),
and the model gives us an output (the response). In the research accessing the API with python
(like proposed here), the prompt is embedded in the python script, alongside other elements of
code, which operate some data manipulation and parsing (like saving the initial codes in a .csv
file). There are different strategies for prompting (see for an overview e.g. Wang et al., 2023), such
zero-shot, few-shots, chain-of-thought. The one in the example relating to chatGPT (Figure 2) is
zero-shot as it does not provide any example on which the model can produce a response, nor does
it show the model how to think toward solving a problem. For TA so far, the author has used a
zero-shot prompting strategy, which therefore relies entirely on the LLM capacity to produce a
downstream output from a set of instructions.
Figure 2 – Example of a simple prompt in chatGPT



For this research two prompts will be used: one for the initial coding (phase 2 of TA) and one for
the identification of themes (phases 3-4 of TA). The prompts that are proposed here will also be
in Italian, to test the capacity of the models used to understand instruction in the same language of
the undelying dataset. One test will also be performed on initial coding (for one interview) using
an equivalent prompt in English to assess whether the model derives similar initial codes when the
instructions are on a different language than the dataset.

Figure 3 shows the prompt for the initial coding used within a python script. The response of the
model to this prompt is captured by the get_completion function. This function has been described
elsewhere (See e.g. Author, 2023) and readers can consult the previous papers. It is anyway a
standard completion function for the GPT LLMs, widely document for example in the OpenAI
website1. This prompt is the equivalent Italian translation of the prompts proposed in Author 2024.
The prompt in Figure 3 (alongside the one shown in Figure 4) has some relevant features,
compared with earlier prompts proposed for TA with LLMs. First, they are generally open-ended
and do not fix in advance the number of initial codes to be generated. Second, the language used
adheres to the jargon of Thematic Analysis, using key words such as ‘initial codes’ and ‘themes’
in the prompts, rather than more vague words such as topics or items. In the prompt each interview
is passed in a variable ({testo}), and then to the model with the prompt (as summarised in Figure
1), which will return a list of initial codes (with names, descriptions and a representative quote),
organised as a codebook. These are returned as JSON objects which are then easy to parse in a
CSV file for the next step of the analysis, the generation of themes. For the generation of themes,
the prompt in Figure 4 was used. The prompt receives the list of all initial codes and asks the model
to generate a series of themes (at least 9, I fixed the themes number to match more or less the
number of categories produced by Galandi et al., (2023) using the LLM generated codes. For
performing phase 4 (the refining of themes), in previous work (see Author 2023a, 2023b) I
proposed to modify the Temperature (T) parameter of the model. Temperature is a parameter that
increases the ‘creativity’ of the LLM in responding to the prompt. Temperature at zero (0) normally

The categories by Gualandi et al. (2022) will be used to assess the results produced by the LLMs.
For doing this I will propose an approach that uses another LLM (the model SBERT, see Thakur
et al., 2022), specifically for semantic similarity. Semantic similarity is an approach to measure
similarity between texts. It is a task of Natural Language Processing that measures the semantic
‘distance’ between two texts. In simpler terms the model will receive two ‘texts’ (e.g. an original
category, and a theme from this research) and will produce a score for similarity or divergence,
essential a score between 1 and –1 (cosine similarity). The closer the score is to 1 then the more
similarity the sentences have. The closer to –1 then their meaning diverges toward being opposite.
A score of 0 means that the texts are not similar at all. Using semantic similarity between the
categories from the original analysis and the TA produced here, we could offer an assessment of
how well the LLM performs with using semi-structured interviews and prompts in Italian. Results
will be shown in a similarity matrix. This approach to assess the quality of the output was used (in
a similar fashion) elsewhere in our work on TA with LLMs (see e.g. Mathis et al., 2024). The same
approach will be used to compare the set of codes for one interview produced with the prompt in
Italian and an equivalent prompt in English. This will be done to assess whether similar or
equivalent codes are produced by the model when working with a prompt in a language different
than the one of the underlying dataset.

A further evaluation will be proposed, based on a human comparative assessment of the themes
and the original categories from Gualandi et al. (2022). For this I contacted the first author of the
original research and asked to compare the LLM themes with their categories and to offer a
qualitative score of similarity between 0-10, or in other words to score how well the themes from
the model capture the meaning of the original categories. Results of this will also be shown in a
matrix graph.

4.2 Themes

Phase 3 of TA encompasses the sorting and organising of initial codes into higher level Themes.
These are the patterns that emerge from the data and in essence the core analytical component of
the TA method. Using the prompt in Figure 3 and passing the list of the initial codes previously
generated as input in the prompt, the model (GPT4-Turbo in this phase) can define these themes.
The prompt request to generate a set of themes with a request to have at least 9. The reason for this
is that the original research produced 8 high-level categories. With 9 themes from the model, we
can better see if some of these themes do appear similar to the original ones, having one extra
theme to play with. Table 3 presents the 9 Themes produced (reported verbatim and in the same
order given by the model, in appendix an English translation produced with chatGPT is provided).
We can see that the model offers themes with a dense description. Examples of codes included in
each theme are reported in the last column. In the appendix the translation of two themes is reported
to allow better judgement of the quality of this output.

   6. Discussion and Conclusion
The problem of this paper was to test whether an inductive Thematic Analysis performed with a
pre-trained Large Language Model can be performed on semi-structured interviews in a language
other than English. For this purpose, a test was conducted using a set of qualitative semi-structured
interviews in Italian. Moreover, for the test, the methodology for TA with LLMs proposed
previously by the author (see e.g. Author, 2023a) was adopted. The question is then, does the LLM
perform a TA which is ‘good enough’? That displays sufficient quality? Even when LLMs work
with material other than English.

Overall, we have seen how the pre-trained models (GPT3.5-Turbo and GPT4-Turbo), can identify
a set of codes from each of the interviews in Italian. These codes do appear of sufficient quality.
The model has identified 185 codes from the 19 interviews. These were then used within a prompt
to instruct the model to identify the themes. In the research we asked to identify 9 themes. These
were then compared with the 8 high-level categories (created with Grounded Theory) produced by
Gualandi et al. (2022, 2023) in their original analysis of the interviews, for semantic similarity.
The LLMs can produce themes which are generally comparable in the large majority of cases.
Only one of the original categories by Gualandi et al. (2022) was never inductively produced by
the model (the category ‘research evaluation’), but the other 7 were generally seen by the LLM,
showing semantic similarity (derived by scoring pairs categories/themes with sbert) with a score
always >0.6. For this test, moreover, the prompts for the LLM were used in the original language
of the dataset. A test for the themes was done with an equivalent prompt in English, showing that
the original language and the English prompt can generally produce similar themes. A further
quality assessment was performed by asking one of the authors of the original research to assign a
score about how well the LLM themes capture the significance and meaning of the original
categories. In this case the scoring also appeared rather high, thus further confirming the LLMs
capacity to perform an inductive qualitative analysis.

This research consolidates the existing initial body of work on using LLMs for TA, which was
discussed in the literature review. There is no direct innovation in the procedure for the analysis
described elsewhere (see e.g. Author 2023; Mathis et al., 2024), however there are important
practical implications coming from this research. First, it shows (at least in one example case –
Italian) that pre-trained LLMs have multilingual capacity to perform the analysis. This would
allow the methodology to be adopted for example in international projects (like European projects)
where often qualitative data (such as semi-structured interviews) is collected in diverse national
languages, supporting a common analysis procedure without the need for translating interview in
English for example, or allowing a common analysis even if individual researchers from different
countries perform their own analysis in parallel. Moreover, as qualitative research seeks to explore
the deep understanding of social issues from the perspective of the actors, the multilingual
capacities of LLMs could allow the methodology to be adopted in multiple linguistic contexts.
This of course, so long that the language is supported by the pre-trained model, in a similar fashion
as in the test with Italian proposed in this work.

In this research we used pre-trained commercial models which are easy to access and (at least for
GPT3.5.-Turbo) are relatively cheap to use. But the scientific community is also fine-tuning Open-
Source models in specific languages. For example, for Italian a large model based on the Llama-2
70b was recently released, called “Llamantino” (Basile et al., 2023). This is instruction tuned for
supporting Italian NLP researchers. However, the general overhead in terms of computing
resources or technical skills needed to run (at least at present) an Open-Source model such as this
are quite high. An Open Source model the size of Llamantino would require dedicated hardware
resources and technical mastery not always available to social scientists. Whilst in the future these
fine-tuned models in a specific language should be tested in their capacity to perform TA (using
the methods discussed earlier), at present the commercially available models do appear easier to
use, and do not require any significant investment in technology, nor they require very advanced
computing skills (beyond some basic python programming). Further incremental research should
possibly test the TA with LLMs as described here with other languages to further validate the
findings. The choice to use Italian was practical (because the author knows the language), and it
was assessed that Italian is still a major European language. Tests may be performed with other
major languages (e.g. German) but possibly also with languages of smaller countries (e.g. Croatian
or Slovak for example).

In conclusion a TA on semi-structured interviews in Italian can be performed with a commercial,
pre-trained LLM. The analysis results do appear of sufficient ‘good quality’, generally with some
comparability to the original research performed by human researchers. Moreover, the outputs
produced by the model do appear similar to those produced in previous research (e.g. Author,
2023) on data in English, also using prompts in English. Performing a TA with LLM in Italian
thus does seem viable, and this contribution potentially opens reflections on a broader multilingual
application of the method.
