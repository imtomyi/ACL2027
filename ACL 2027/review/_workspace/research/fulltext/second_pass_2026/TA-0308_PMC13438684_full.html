ABSTRACT

Human–artificial intelligence (AI) teams are increasingly embedded in risk analysis, yet news reports repeatedly portray meaningful oversight as fragile when review conditions are poorly designed. This study examines how public discourse has portrayed the roles, failure modes, and governance mechanisms of human–AI teaming in risk analysis. Drawing on 184,282 AI‐related Factiva news articles (1956–2025), we use a funnel‐shaped mixed‐methods design to identify 3571 articles describing human–AI interaction in risk‐analytic contexts. Structural topic modeling identifies 20 thematic clusters and their temporal and cross‐domain dynamics, whereas LLM‐assisted inductive thematic analysis produces five mirror‐mapped pairs of failure modes and governance mechanisms: opacity and explainability, bias and auditing, agency erosion and cognitive friction, systemic fragility and oversight and liability, and accountability vacuums and institutional governance. The analysis shows that media discourse often follows an AI‐predicts‐human‐decides paradigm and portrays formal human‐in‐the‐loop requirements as insufficient without deliberate interaction design.

Keywords: governance mechanism, human–AI teaming, media discourse, risk analysis, structural topic modeling

1. Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) is becoming deeply embedded in risk analysis that affects health, safety, finance, and governance (Gonzalez et al. 2026; Stødle et al. 2025; Tang et al. 2026; Zhong and Xu 2026). In medical diagnostics, AI systems assist clinicians in identifying pathological patterns that exceed the speed and consistency of unassisted human review (Rajpurkar et al. 2022; Topol 2019). In criminal justice, algorithmic risk assessment instruments generate recidivism predictions that inform pretrial detention and sentencing decisions (Dressel and Farid 2018). In autonomous driving, AI perception systems continuously monitor road conditions, but human drivers must remain prepared to resume control when the system encounters conditions beyond its operational design domain (Liang et al. 2025). Across these and other high‐stakes domains, human–AI teams are replacing purely human or purely algorithmic decision‐making, with the expectation that combining human judgment with AI pattern recognition will yield superior outcomes (Dell'Acqua et al. 2026; Gonzalez et al. 2026).

However, this integration introduces interaction risks that neither traditional human factors research nor conventional AI safety frameworks fully address. Automation bias leads human operators to defer uncritically to algorithmic recommendations (Mosier and Skitka 1996; Parasuraman and Manzey 2010), whereas algorithm aversion causes individuals to disproportionately reject algorithmic guidance after observing even minor errors (Dietvorst et al. 2015). These opposing tendencies complicate efforts to calibrate human trust in AI, a challenge that complementarity frameworks have addressed by emphasizing reasoning, memory, attention, and meta‐coordination (Gonzalez et al. 2026; Gonzalez and Heidari 2025). The core tension is that risk analysis, because of epistemic uncertainty and value trade‐offs, requires a risk‐informed decision logic in which meaningful human judgment remains necessary (Stødle et al. 2025), yet reported cases suggest that the review conditions needed to preserve meaningful oversight are often fragile. Insurance claim reviews completed in 1.2 s (Rucker and Armstrong 2024), judicial reliance on opaque recidivism scores (Angwin et al. 2016), and repeated failures in autonomous vehicle handover (Endsley 2017) illustrate reported pathways through which human‐in‐the‐loop governance can devolve into a compliance ritual (Green 2022).

Despite important advances in theorizing human–AI complementarity (Gonzalez et al. 2026), documenting trust calibration failures in laboratory settings (Bansal et al. 2021; Dietvorst et al. 2015), and conceptualizing AI integration within risk analysis (Stødle et al. 2025), three research gaps persist. First, existing studies are largely confined to single domains or small‐scale samples (Lu and Zhang 2025; Wang et al. 2026), and lack cross‐domain evidence on how human–AI teaming in risk analysis is publicly narrated across application contexts, leaving the discourse‐level generalizability of complementarity frameworks underexamined (Gonzalez et al. 2026). Second, although news media play a recognized role in shaping public perception of emerging technologies (Entman 1993; Ittefaq et al. 2025), and prior work has tracked the evolution of AI public perception through media analysis (Denia 2025; Fast and Horvitz 2017), no study has systematically leveraged news data to examine how the challenges of human–AI teaming in risk analysis are portrayed in public discourse. Third, existing AI risk taxonomies tend to be deductive, deriving categories from theory (e.g., Weidinger et al. 2022), leaving a gap for inductive research that allows publicly reported failure modes and governance mechanisms to emerge from large‐scale textual evidence. To address these gaps, this study poses three research questions (RQs):

  • RQ1

    (role evolution): How have the roles assigned to humans and AI in risk analysis evolved over time and varied across application domains?

  • RQ2

    (thematic landscape): What dominant themes characterize portrayals of human–AI teaming in risk analysis, and how have these thematic patterns shifted across time periods and domains?

  • RQ3

    (failure modes and governance): What failure modes and governance mechanisms for human–AI teaming in risk analysis emerge inductively from large‐scale textual data, and how do they map onto actionable intervention strategies?

We analyze a Factiva search window from 1956 to 2025, with substantive empirical concentration after 2015, using a funnel‐shaped mixed‐methods design. News media serve as a valuable data source because media reporting tends to converge with public opinion, whether by shaping it, reflecting it, or both (Nisbet 2009), and media framing selectively highlights certain aspects of perceived reality to promote particular problem definitions and causal interpretations (Entman 1993). Using the Factiva database, we constructed an initial corpus of 184,282 AI‐related news articles, refined through risk dictionary filtering and LLM‐assisted screening to obtain 3571 articles substantively describing human–AI teaming in risk‐analytic contexts. We then applied structural topic modeling (STM) for macro‐level thematic discovery (RQ1 and RQ2) and LLM‐assisted inductive thematic analysis for micro‐level mechanism identification (RQ3).

This study makes three contributions. First, it provides cross‐domain, discourse‐based empirical evidence across 12 application domains, showing how complementarity, oversight, and governance failure are publicly narrated in news media (Gonzalez et al. 2026). The analysis identifies an AI‐predicts‐human‐decides media portrayal and a post‐2022 shift in topic salience that coincides with the rise of generative AI coverage. Second, through inductive analysis that imposes no predetermined coding categories, this study identifies five pairs of mirror‐mapped failure modes and governance mechanisms: actionable explainability, algorithmic auditing, cognitive friction design, oversight and liability, and institutional governance. Third, the methodological integration of LLM‐assisted text screening and open coding within a mixed‐methods design provides a replicable template for large‐scale textual research in risk analysis.

2. Literature Review

2.1. Human–AI Teaming and the Complementarity Challenge in Risk Analysis

The concept of human–AI teaming in risk analysis builds on human factors research on automation, joint activity, and trust calibration. Early work decomposed human–machine interaction into information‐processing stages and levels of automation (Parasuraman et al. 2000), whereas later work emphasized predictability, directability, and mutual intelligibility in joint activity (Klein et al. 2004). The use, misuse, disuse, and abuse framework provided an early vocabulary for trust calibration failures (Parasuraman and Riley 1997). Building on these foundations, complementarity theory holds that human–AI teams outperform either party alone only when collaboration is deliberately designed rather than assumed to emerge from co‐location in a workflow (Gonzalez et al. 2026). A two‐dimensional framework treating human control and automation as independent further rejects the assumption that greater automation necessarily entails reduced human agency (Shneiderman 2022). Within risk analysis, epistemic uncertainty and value trade‐offs mean that decisions must remain risk‐informed rather than risk‐based, requiring human judgment to remain meaningful (Stødle et al. 2025; Thekdi et al. 2025).

However, empirical research consistently demonstrates that this ideal complementarity is difficult to achieve in practice. Automation bias, the tendency of human operators to defer uncritically to automated systems, manifests as omission errors when automation fails to alert and commission errors when operators follow incorrect machine advice (Mosier and Skitka 1996; Parasuraman and Manzey 2010). Algorithm aversion, by contrast, leads individuals to disproportionately abandon algorithms after observing errors, even when the algorithm outperforms human judgment overall (Dietvorst et al. 2015), though the counterpart phenomenon of algorithm appreciation has been documented in certain contexts (Logg et al. 2019), indicating that trust calibration is highly context‐dependent. Three challenges for AI‐assisted decision‐making have been synthesized from this literature: the matching problem (determining when to rely on AI), the learning problem (calibrating trust over time), and the scaling problem (generalizing effective collaboration patterns across domains) (Steyvers and Kumar 2024). Crucially, providing AI explanations does not automatically produce complementary team performance (Bansal et al. 2021), and a survey of over 100 empirical studies confirmed that explainability alone is not a reliable predictor of improved collaborative outcomes (Lai et al. 2023). These findings indicate that the core obstacle to effective human–AI teaming lies not in AI capability but in the design of the interaction itself.

The consequences of these trust calibration failures vary substantially across application domains. Although clinical time pressures in healthcare risk reducing AI oversight to a formality (Rajpurkar et al. 2022; Topol 2019), algorithmic risk assessments in criminal justice often induce systematic judicial reliance despite accuracy disparities (Angwin et al. 2016; Dressel and Farid 2018). Similarly, autonomous driving suffers from attention degradation during handover protocols (Endsley 2017). Although recent studies have explored these dynamics within single domains—such as medical AI frameworks (Wang et al. 2026) or micro‐lending experiments showing that AI explanations only work when triggering active rethinking (Lu and Zhang 2025)—systematic cross‐domain comparisons remain underdeveloped.

2.2. The Oversight Illusion and the Limits of Current Governance

To address the trust calibration and interaction design challenges documented above, regulatory frameworks increasingly mandate human oversight of high‐risk AI systems. The European Union AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689) Article 14 distinguishes among human‐in‐the‐loop, human‐on‐the‐loop, and human‐in‐command modalities and includes provisions against automation bias (Article 14(4)(b)). However, three fundamental flaws have been identified in such policies: automation bias causes reviewers to defer to algorithmic outputs, vague enforcement standards lack operational specificity, and the label of human oversight creates a false sense of security (Green 2022). The notion of meaningful human control requires that control extend beyond the binary presence of a human decision‐maker to encompass the conditions under which informed judgment can be exercised (Crootof 2016; Gonzalez et al. 2026). When these conditions are absent, human‐in‐the‐loop becomes what may be termed an oversight illusion: humans are formally responsible for decisions they lack the capacity to meaningfully evaluate.

The academic community has proposed several governance mechanisms to counteract this illusion, though each carries limitations. The explainable AI literature has produced a rich methodological repertoire (Barredo Arrieta et al. 2020), yet post hoc explanations may not faithfully represent the model's actual decision logic, and high‐stakes decisions should employ inherently interpretable models (Rudin 2019). A double standard has been noted in which algorithmic decision‐making is held to transparency requirements far exceeding those applied to human judgment (Zerilli et al. 2019). On accountability, the concept of the responsibility gap observes that for learning automata, responsibility cannot be fully ascribed to any single party (Matthias 2004), a gap that has widened as AI systems have grown more autonomous. In auditing, an end‐to‐end framework for internal algorithmic auditing provides an operational blueprint for shifting from reactive remediation to proactive prevention (Raji et al. 2020), whereas the phenomenon of disparate interactions demonstrates that human–algorithm combination can produce bias that neither would produce in isolation (Green and Chen 2019). Among the most promising emerging interventions, cognitive forcing functions draw on dual‐process theory (Kahneman 2011) to compel analytical thinking before human decision‐makers accept AI recommendations (Buçinca et al. 2021). Clinical evidence from radiology supports this approach: requiring clinicians to form a provisional judgment before viewing AI suggestions reduced agreement with AI output (Fogliato et al. 2022). Granting humans the ability to modify algorithmic outputs, even slightly, overcomes algorithm aversion (Dietvorst et al. 2018), and attention orchestration, in which humans are trained to interrogate rather than passively accept AI output, has been identified as critical for team performance (Gonzalez et al. 2026).

Despite these advances, current AI risk taxonomies and governance frameworks are predominantly deductive, with limited inductive evidence on how failure modes are publicly described across domains. Frameworks such as the sociotechnical harm taxonomy (Shelby et al. 2023) and the LLM risk taxonomy (Weidinger et al. 2022) derive categories from theory, specifying what risks should exist rather than documenting how failure patterns are publicly described across domains. Moreover, cognitive friction interventions remain predominantly validated in laboratory and clinical settings, and large‐scale evidence of their adoption in organizational practice is lacking.

In summary, while the theoretical foundations of human–AI complementarity and meaningful human control are well‐established, the empirical literature remains constrained by three critical gaps. First, existing validations of human–AI teaming are largely fragmented into single‐domain studies or controlled laboratory experiments, lacking cross‐domain evidence on how collaboration roles and interaction risks are represented at a societal level. Second, although public discourse serves as the primary arena where the boundaries of algorithmic authority and human accountability are negotiated, no study has systematically leveraged large‐scale media‐text evidence to examine the thematic landscape of human–AI teaming challenges in risk analysis. Third, current AI risk taxonomies are predominantly deductive, leaving room for inductive analysis of publicly reported failure modes and their corresponding governance mechanisms in news discourse. To address these gaps, this study uses large‐scale news media data to provide cross‐domain discourse mapping and an inductively derived typology that connects failure diagnosis to governance intervention.

3. Methodology

We employed a funnel‐shaped, multistage mixed‐methods design (Figure 1) that progressively narrows the analytical focus from a broad corpus to targeted thematic analysis. Stage 1 combines LLM‐assisted screening with structured variable extraction and descriptive statistics to characterize the evolving roles of humans and AI in risk analysis (RQ1). Stage 2 applies STM to identify the macro‐level thematic landscape and its temporal and cross‐domain dynamics (RQ2). Stage 3 uses LLM‐assisted inductive thematic analysis to extract failure modes and governance mechanisms (RQ3). The funnel‐shaped screening pipeline therefore ends at the validated human–AI risk corpus: 184,282 articles in the initial corpus, 16,205 after risk dictionary filtering, and 3,571 after LLM screening.

3.1. Data Collection and Preprocessing

Data were collected from the Factiva database (Dow Jones), one of the world's largest news and business information repositories, covering more than 33,000 sources across over 200 countries. Factiva has been widely used for large‐scale media analysis (e.g., Baghersad and Zobel 2022; Taeuscher and Rothe 2024). We selected “Major News and Business Sources” to ensure quality. The data collection period spanned from January 1, 1956, to September 30, 2025, encompassing the full history of AI as a recognized field of research and application. The search was restricted to English‐language records, and no additional geographic restriction was imposed beyond source availability and English‐language filtering.

Following prior media‐framing studies of AI news coverage that use broad AI‐related keyword strategies (Ittefaq et al. 2025; Nguyen and Hekman 2022; Watson and Wozniak‐O'Connor 2025), the search strategy employed seven AI‐related keyword groups: “artificial intelligence,” “machine learning,” “deep learning,” “neural network,” “generative AI,” “large language model,” and “artificial general intelligence.” Search terms were required to appear in the headline or lead paragraph, a restriction intended to ensure that AI was central to the article rather than only mentioned in passing (Weathers et al. 2025; Zhang and Ha 2023). Exclusion criteria removed republished news, recurring pricing and market data, obituaries, sports, calendars, and other non‐substantive content. After deduplication, the initial corpus comprised 184,282 news articles.

To evaluate whether the original search underrepresented earlier AI‐adjacent systems, we also conducted a search‐stage sensitivity analysis using adjacent terms including “expert system*,” “automated decision system*,” “decision support,” “predictive analytic*,” “data mining,” and “algorithmic decision‐making.” Under the same Factiva conditions and full‐text deduplication, these adjacent terms added 6060 net English‐language articles. Applying the same screening procedures to these supplemental records retained 102 additional relevant articles, equivalent to 2.86% of the 3571‐article final corpus, indicating limited additional coverage under this sensitivity check. The exact query, restrictions, exclusions, and sensitivity results are reported in Supporting Information Appendix A.

To identify articles with risk‐related content, we applied the risk terminology dictionary developed by Bryce et al. (2025), which is based on the 2018 Society for Risk Analysis (SRA) Glossary of risk terms. Retention required at least one mention of the term “risk” as well as the mention of at least one additional risk‐related keyword from the dictionary in the article text (Bryce et al. 2025), yielding 16,205 risk‐related AI news articles. This filtering step reduced the corpus by approximately 91%, concentrating subsequent analyses on the intersection of AI and risk discourse.

3.2. LLM‐Assisted Screening and Extraction

The 16,205 risk‐related articles included substantial noise: articles addressing AI‐related market risk, speculative technology forecasts, and other content that did not substantively describe human–AI interaction in risk‐analytic contexts. To isolate the target population, we conducted LLM‐assisted screening using Qwen3.5‐27b, with concurrent processing. LLM‐based screening was adopted because the concept of “human–AI teaming” involves complex semantic relationships (e.g., implicit divisions of labor, collaborative workflows, and oversight arrangements) that simple keyword co‐occurrence filters cannot reliably capture, thereby reducing false positives relative to rule‐based approaches. This model was selected for its balance of strong instruction‐following capability and cost efficiency, providing stable JSON output formatting and sufficient semantic comprehension for the classification task. The LLM was configured with a temperature of 0 to ensure deterministic output. The complete screening prompt is provided in Supporting Information Appendix A.

The screening prompt comprised two tasks. Task 1 (binary classification) asked whether the article substantively described human–AI teaming in risk assessment, prediction, or decision‐making. Task 2 (open‐ended variable extraction) was conditional on an affirmative classification and extracted three structured variables: application domain, human role, and AI role. Critically, Task 2 employed an open‐ended extraction strategy with no predefined category lists, paralleling open coding in qualitative research (Braun and Clarke 2006).

The screening yielded 3571 articles (22.0% pass rate), producing 1200 unique domain values, 2210 unique human role values, and 2604 unique AI role values. To validate screening accuracy, we randomly sampled 300 articles from the 16,205 risk‐related articles (150 classified by the LLM as relevant and 150 as irrelevant) for independent manual verification by three researchers. Each researcher independently performed binary classification on every sample, and the final human label was determined by majority vote with adjudication of ambiguous cases. Agreement among the three human coders was very high for the binary screening task, with Fleiss’ kappa = 0.976. Compared with the adjudicated human labels, the LLM screening achieved 96.3% accuracy, 94.7% precision, 97.9% recall, 94.8% specificity, an F1 score of 96.3%, and Cohen's kappa of 0.927. Because the validation sample was balanced, we estimated prevalence‐adjusted predictive values using the observed corpus pass rate of 22.0%, yielding an estimated positive predictive value (PPV) of 84.3% and negative predictive value (NPV) of 99.4%. These results suggest that the LLM screening procedure provided a reliable basis for isolating the target corpus while maintaining a very low false‐negative risk.

Because the free‐form labels were too dispersed for analytical use, three researchers independently consolidated the high‐frequency labels into bounded category systems, guided by domain knowledge. Initial independent coding was followed by iterative discussion to resolve disagreements, producing a consensus category scheme. The final scheme comprises 12 domain categories (healthcare, finance, cybersecurity, defense, justice, governance, industry, transportation, media, environment, labor, and education), 5 human role categories (decision‐maker, reviewer, supervisor, analyst, and operator), and 6 AI role categories (predictor, detector, advisor, screener, executor, and generator). We separately validated these three structured variables using a random sample of 300 articles from the 3571 screened articles. Agreement among the three human coders was also very high for structured‐variable coding, with Fleiss’ kappa values of 0.975 for domain, 0.969 for human role, and 0.980 for AI role. Exact agreement with adjudicated human labels was 96.0% for domain (κ = 0.952), 95.7% for human role (κ = 0.925), and 95.0% for AI role (κ = 0.933), indicating that the extraction and consolidation procedure was sufficiently reliable for the descriptive and STM analyses. Full validation tables are reported in Supporting Information Appendix B.

For analytical consistency, each article was assigned a single primary category for each structured variable. For application domain, the primary category reflected the main risk decision context in the article. For human role, classification prioritized the actor's primary responsibility in the workflow, distinguishing final decision authority, output review, system supervision, analytical interpretation, and direct operation. For AI role, classification prioritized the system's main function in the described risk workflow, distinguishing prediction, detection, recommendation, screening, autonomous execution, and generation. Ambiguous and compound labels were adjudicated using these priority rules, with illustrative cases reported in Supporting Information Appendix B.

3.3. Structural Topic Modeling

STM was applied to the 3571 screened articles to identify latent thematic structures and their variation across time and domains (Roberts et al. 2019). Text preprocessing followed standard procedures: lowercasing, stopword removal, number and punctuation removal, and stemming, with a minimum word length of three characters and terms appearing in fewer than 10 documents excluded. The number of topics (K) was selected using the searchK function, evaluating K = 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30 across semantic coherence, exclusivity, held‐out likelihood, and residual dispersion. K = 20 was selected as a balance between diagnostic performance and interpretability. Relative to K = 15, K = 20 improved exclusivity (9.359 vs. 9.222), held‐out likelihood (−7.161 vs. −7.189), and residual dispersion (2.136 vs. 2.276), while avoiding the greater semantic‐coherence loss observed at K = 25 and K = 30. Diagnostic plots, numeric searchK results, and K = 15/K = 25 robustness checks are provided in Supporting Information Appendix C.

The prevalence formula was ∼s(year)+domain_cat, with spectral initialization, a maximum of 150 expectation‐maximization (EM) iterations, and a random seed of 42. Topic labeling was performed by two researchers who independently examined frequency‐exclusivity (FREX) words, highest probability words, and representative high‐proportion articles, with labels reconciled through discussion. Complete topic word lists and representative labeling examples appear in Supporting Information Appendix D. Two topics were identified as noise: T14 (financial disclosure) and T17 (public discourse).

3.4. LLM‐Assisted Inductive Thematic Analysis

The third stage addressed RQ3 by applying inductive thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke 2006) to all 3571 articles in the validated human–AI risk corpus. This design captures governance‐relevant cases from domain‐specific topics, including autonomous vehicles, healthcare treatment, medical diagnosis, enterprise security, cyber threats, and physical monitoring.

3.4.1. Phase 1: LLM Open Coding

Qwen3.5‐27b generated open‐ended responses to two core questions for each article: (1) What problems, failures, or concerns about human–AI teaming does this article describe? (2) What solutions, mechanisms, or institutional arrangements does this article describe or propose to improve human oversight of AI? The complete prompt is provided in Supporting Information Appendix E. The 3571 articles produced 16,422 problem phrases (15,567 unique) and 16,683 solution phrases (16,088 unique). Because this inductive stage generated open‐ended phrases without assigning articles to mutually exclusive categories, coding fidelity was assessed through researcher audit and consensus adjudication of sampled article‐level outputs, with the phrase‐to‐code aggregation rules, representative phrases, and distributional summaries reported in Supporting Information Appendix E.

3.4.2. Phase 2: Human Inductive Theme Development

The research team organized the LLM‐generated phrases following the six‐phase thematic analysis framework of Braun and Clarke (2006), yielding a three‐level hierarchy: 30 initial codes, 15 sub‐themes, and 5 overarching themes. Problem and solution phrases were coded separately at the open‐coding and initial‐code stages. The mirror‐mapped structure was introduced during axial interpretation after the separate sub‐theme frameworks had converged, creating a diagnostic‐to‐intervention logic.

4. Findings

4.1. Descriptive Overview: Roles and Domains (RQ1)

4.1.1. Temporal Distribution

Figure 2 presents both the annual volume of screened human–AI risk articles and the same count normalized by annual AI‐related coverage in Factiva. The distribution is heavily right‐skewed: only 17 articles (0.5%) predate 2015, making the pre‐2015 records primarily a historical search boundary. Between 2015 and 2022, the annual count grew from 19 to 164 articles, whereas the normalized share rose from 1.13% to 1.95% of same‐year AI‐related coverage. Articles published between 2023 and 2025 account for 2235 of the 3571 total (62.6%), but the normalized share remained within a narrower range of 1.80%–2.21%. This pattern indicates that the post‐2022 increase partly reflects the sharp expansion of AI‐related media coverage, while still showing that human–AI risk articles remained a visible subset of the broader AI news agenda.

4.1.2. Domain Distribution

Healthcare dominates the corpus with 1004 articles (28.1%), reflecting the density and visibility of human–AI interaction in clinical decision‐making. Finance follows with 718 articles (20.1%), driven by the tension between algorithmic risk scoring and human judgment. Cybersecurity (435 articles, 12.2%) and defense (312 articles, 8.7%) represent security‐critical domains in which human–AI trust and control authority are especially salient. The temporal evolution of domain coverage (Figure 3) reveals a dynamic landscape. Healthcare has consistently maintained the largest share of media attention, experiencing a pronounced peak in 2020 that may coincide with heightened reporting on AI during the COVID‐19 pandemic. Finance saw a substantial increase in early reporting (around 2016) before stabilizing into a consistent share. Conversely, domains such as governance and justice, which were nearly absent from the discourse in 2015, have steadily expanded their footprint in recent years. This trend reflects a broadening of human–AI teaming discourse: As AI deployment matures, media attention has expanded beyond traditional operational sectors (like healthcare and finance) to encompass structural and institutional risk domains.

4.1.3. Role Distribution

Among human roles, decision‐maker is the most frequent category (60.0%); among AI roles, predictor predominates (40.5%) (Figure 4). This distribution indicates that the dominant paradigm in media portrayals of human–AI teaming in risk analysis is the AI‐predicts, human‐decides model, in which AI generates probabilistic assessments and humans exercise final judgment. This paradigm aligns with the complementarity design in which AI serves as a low‐agency advisor and humans retain ethical authority (Gonzalez et al. 2026). However, in the corpus this configuration is also portrayed as vulnerable to oversight illusions: When decision‐makers lack the time, information, or cognitive resources to interrogate AI output, the decision role can become an approval function.

4.1.4. Role Interactions

The cross‐tabulation of human and AI roles (Figure 5) confirms decision‐maker × predictor as the most frequent pairing. Two other notable pairings emerge: reviewer × detector (supervisory detection scenarios such as compliance review) and supervisor × executor (autonomous system oversight). These three patterns represent qualitatively distinct configurations of authority, agency, and risk exposure.

4.2. Structural Topic Model: Thematic Landscape (RQ2)

4.2.1. Topic Overview

The 20‐topic STM solution reveals a thematic landscape organized around domain‐specific risk concerns, cross‐cutting governance issues, and emergent technology‐driven themes. Figure 6 presents the distribution of topic proportions. Topic labels were checked against representative high‐proportion articles and adjacent‐topic distinctions, particularly T3 versus T13, T7 versus T15, and T6 versus T9. Two noise topics (T14, financial disclosure; T17, public discourse) are included for completeness but excluded from substantive interpretation.

4.2.2. Domain‐Topic Associations

The domain‐topic association heatmap (Figure 7) reveals how different application domains face distinct configurations of risk in human–AI teaming.

Healthcare articles concentrate on T3 (healthcare treatment) and T13 (medical diagnosis), reflecting concerns about efficiency‐driven erosion of clinical review. Finance articles are dominated by T19 (banking fraud), where human reviewers face information asymmetry in evaluating AI‐generated fraud alerts. Cybersecurity articles cluster around T7 (enterprise security) and T15 (cyber threats), reflecting alert fatigue when security analysts must adjudicate high volumes of AI‐generated threat notifications. Defense articles are concentrated on T2 (military weapons), centered on whether human control can be preserved over lethal autonomous systems. Justice articles span T11 (policing surveillance) and T20 (legal system), addressing predictive policing and algorithmic risk assessment in sentencing. Media articles concentrate on T10 (social media safety), reflecting the challenges between AI content moderation systems and human reviewers. Two topics exhibit substantial cross‐domain presence: T6 (AI regulation) and T9 (generative AI), indicating that governance concerns and generative AI challenges transcend individual application contexts.

4.2.3. Temporal Dynamics

Figure 8 presents observed changes in topic proportions before and after the public release of ChatGPT, using 2023 as the descriptive threshold year. Topics with the largest increases include T9 (generative AI, +7.3%), T4 (digital transformation, +5.6%), and T6 (AI regulation, +2.2%). This shift coincides with new human–AI interaction modalities in which humans are increasingly portrayed as verifying generative content rather than evaluating only predictive recommendations, alongside a growing demand for regulatory frameworks.

Conversely, topics that experienced the sharpest declines include T7 (enterprise security, −4.5%), T12 (AI existential risk, −3.8%), and T16 (physical monitoring, −2.4%). Meanwhile, topics, such as T5 (tech investment) and T10 (social media safety), remained relatively stable (changes of <0.5%). In the post‐2022 period, public attention shifted from abstract, hypothetical threats (e.g., AI existential risk) and traditional surveillance tasks (e.g., physical monitoring) toward the immediate challenges of integrating and regulating generative models in everyday workflows.

4.3. Inductive Thematic Analysis: Failure Modes and Governance Mechanisms (RQ3)

4.3.1. Overview of the Thematic Framework

Inductive thematic analysis of the full 3571‐article human–AI risk corpus produced a three‐level coding framework comprising 30 initial codes nested within 15 sub‐themes and 5 overarching themes. Tables 1 and 2 present the problem and solution frameworks. The problem themes are paired with corresponding solution themes at the interpretive level: algorithmic opacity maps to actionable explainability, amplified bias to algorithmic auditing, erosion of human agency to cognitive friction design, systemic fragility to oversight and liability, and accountability vacuums to institutional governance. Supporting Information Appendix E reports theme frequencies, domain and time distributions, and the problem–solution co‐occurrence matrix. In frequency terms, systemic fragility was the most prevalent problem theme (1544 articles, 43.2%), followed by accountability vacuums (1362 articles, 38.1%) and erosion of human agency (1122 articles, 31.4%).

4.3.2. Problem Themes: Five Failure Modes

4.3.2.1. Theme P1: Algorithmic Opacity

This theme encompasses black‐box decisions, unreliable explanations, and AI hallucinations. When AI systems serve as predictors or advisors in risk analysis and their reasoning processes are opaque to human decision‐makers, the corpus portrays the collaborative relationship as shifting from bidirectional interaction toward unidirectional information transfer. Opacity operates at three escalating levels: black‐box decisions render the basis for AI judgments entirely invisible; post hoc explanations may not faithfully represent the model's actual decision logic, creating a false sense of understanding; and generative AI hallucinations present reviewers with actively misleading content. As one news report described, a physician tasked with reviewing an AI‐generated insurance claim denial could not access any explanation of the factors that drove the decision, leaving her unable to evaluate whether the denial was clinically justified. Across all three levels, the common consequence is that human reviewers are portrayed as unable to perform meaningful interrogation of AI output, a precondition for the complementary collaboration that effective risk analysis requires.

4.3.2.2. Theme P2: Amplified Bias and Inequity

This theme comprises discriminatory outcomes, biased training data, and uncritical deployment. Bias in human–AI risk analysis originates not solely from the model but from the interaction between algorithmic outputs and human decision‐making processes. In these reports, weak scrutiny of training data and post‐deployment fairness allows bias to scale through institutional deployment. In criminal justice, for example, media coverage documented how recidivism prediction tools flagged Black defendants as high risk at nearly twice the rate of white defendants with comparable histories, while judges reviewing the scores rarely questioned the underlying data. Human reviewers, confronted with large volumes of seemingly objective risk scores, tend to attribute authority to quantitative precision and to overlook systematic error.

4.3.2.3. Theme P3: Erosion of Human Agency

This theme encompasses rubber‐stamping, cognitive deskilling, and impaired review conditions. Human agency erodes through three reinforcing mechanisms. Rubber‐stamping occurs when institutional pressures reduce the human review function to automatic approval of AI output. As one widely reported investigation revealed, medical advisors at an insurance company were approving AI‐generated claim denials at an average rate of 1.2 s per case, a pace portrayed as incompatible with substantive evaluation. Cognitive deskilling describes the atrophy of independent analytical capability resulting from prolonged reliance on AI‐assisted decision‐making, creating a vicious cycle in which dependence on AI further diminishes the capacity to evaluate AI critically. Impaired review conditions describe system design features that actively obstruct meaningful human oversight, such as information overload, poor interface design, and unrealistic time allocations.

4.3.2.4. Theme P4: Systemic Fragility

This theme includes loss of control, harm to vulnerable groups, and hand‐over failures. Human–AI systems in risk analysis may perform adequately under normal conditions but exhibit pronounced fragility in the face of edge cases, adversarial inputs, or distributional shifts. A recurring high‐consequence interaction pattern is hand‐over failure: when an AI system encounters conditions beyond its operational capability and attempts to transfer control to a human operator whose situational awareness has degraded. As reported in multiple autonomous vehicle incidents, AI sensors failed to classify unusual road obstacles, triggering handover alerts to drivers who had not been actively monitoring the road. The consequences of systemic failure fall disproportionately on vulnerable populations with less power to contest algorithmic decisions.

4.3.2.5. Theme P5: Accountability Vacuums

This theme encompasses regulatory lag, responsibility diffusion, and data governance breaches. When humans and AI jointly participate in risk‐critical decisions, responsibility diffuses across developers, deployers, regulators, and end users, creating reported situations in which accountability becomes unclear after adverse outcomes. In one illustrative case reported by multiple outlets, when an AI‐assisted diagnostic system contributed to a misdiagnosis, the hospital attributed the error to the software vendor, the vendor pointed to the clinician's failure to override the recommendation, and the patient was left without a clear avenue for accountability. Legal and professional standards have not adapted to the novel responsibility structures created by human–AI teaming in risk analysis.

4.3.3. Governance Mechanisms: Five Solution Themes

4.3.3.1. Theme S1: Actionable Explainability (Mirrors P1)

This theme comprises content disclosure, reasoning transparency, and data provenance. Effective explainability in the context of human–AI risk analysis depends on giving reviewers targeted information that supports independent judgment. Content disclosure ensures that humans can identify which elements of a risk assessment are AI‐generated. Reasoning transparency provides traceable decision chains that enable reviewers to examine the basis for specific AI recommendations. Data provenance allows human analysts to evaluate the quality and relevance of the knowledge base underlying AI judgments. These mechanisms collectively transform explainability from a post hoc technical feature into an operational component of the collaborative workflow.

4.3.3.2. Theme S2: Algorithmic Auditing (Mirrors P2)

This theme encompasses independent certification, bias mitigation, and hard guardrails. The governance response to amplified bias involves a shift from reactive remediation to proactive institutional safeguards. Independent third‐party auditing ensures that AI systems undergo bias detection before deployment and continuous fairness monitoring during operation. Hard guardrails establish behavioral boundaries that AI systems cannot exceed, regardless of the patterns present in training data. These mechanisms address the interaction‐level source of bias documented by Green and Chen (2019), who showed that human–algorithm interaction itself can generate disparate impacts.

4.3.3.3. Theme S3: Cognitive Friction Design (Mirrors P3)

This theme includes cognitive forcing, dynamic escalation, and review delays. Cognitive forcing asks human decision‐makers to articulate an independent assessment before viewing AI recommendations. Dynamic escalation adjusts the level of human involvement based on risk severity and AI uncertainty, directing more intensive review to cases where it is most needed. Review delays introduce waiting periods for high‐stakes decisions so that temporal pressure does not preclude reflective judgment. Together, these mechanisms define workflow conditions under which human review can remain independent and deliberative.

4.3.3.4. Theme S4: Oversight and Liability (Mirrors P4)

This theme comprises HITL (human‐in‐the‐loop) mandates, expert verification, and liability assignment. Addressing systemic fragility requires governance mechanisms that convert formal human‐in‐the‐loop requirements into substantive oversight arrangements. This includes institutional guarantees of human final decision authority in critical scenarios, independent expert verification procedures for AI‐generated assessments, and clear legal attribution of liability that creates accountability incentives aligned with the actual distribution of decision‐making authority in the human–AI system.

4.3.3.5. Theme S5: Institutional Governance (Mirrors P5)

This theme encompasses multilateral frameworks, cross‐border collaboration, and organizational governance. Filling accountability vacuums requires multi‐level institutional arrangements that extend beyond the technology and process level. International AI governance frameworks establish shared principles and standards. Cross‐border regulatory cooperation addresses the transnational character of AI deployment. Organizational governance structures, including internal AI ethics committees and review processes, embed accountability within the institutions that deploy human–AI systems.

5. Discussion

5.1. Theoretical Contributions

This study makes three theoretical contributions. First, it extends human–AI complementarity theory by adding a discourse‐based perspective to workflow‐centered accounts of collaboration (Gonzalez et al. 2026). Across 12 application domains, news coverage commonly portrays AI as supplying analytical input, whereas humans retain accountable judgment. This decision‐maker × predictor pattern shows how public narratives allocate epistemic authority, moral responsibility, and control between human and AI actors. The post‐2022 shift in topic salience adds a temporal dimension to this contribution: in this corpus, public attention increasingly centers on generative AI, digital transformation, and AI regulation, suggesting that complementarity discourse is shaped not only by role allocation within decision workflows but also by changing media attention to emerging AI capabilities and governance concerns. Together, these findings link the matching, learning, and scaling problems of AI‐assisted decision‐making to the societal language through which human–AI roles become intelligible and contested across domains and time (Steyvers and Kumar 2024).

Second, the five failure modes provide a bottom‐up typology of publicly reported interaction risks. This typology complements deductive taxonomies of sociotechnical and LLM harms by showing how risk is narrated at the interface of human reviewers, AI systems, and institutional workflows (Shelby et al. 2023; Weidinger et al. 2022). The reported failures link algorithmic opacity, bias amplification, erosion of human agency, systemic fragility, and accountability vacuums into a broader interaction problem. This framing extends AI risk analysis beyond model‐centered harm categories and clarifies that meaningful oversight depends on the informational, temporal, cognitive, and organizational conditions under which human judgment is exercised.

Third, the mirror‐mapped pairing of failure modes with governance mechanisms bridges diagnosis and intervention. Because problem and solution phrases were coded separately, the pairing reflects axial interpretation after the two frameworks had converged. The resulting framework translates failure patterns into governance design questions: Actionable explainability concerns the information humans need to evaluate AI outputs; algorithmic auditing concerns monitoring bias, error, and drift; cognitive friction design concerns preserving independent judgment; oversight and liability concerns authority, escalation, and responsibility; and institutional governance concerns sustaining safeguards across cases and organizations. Together, these mechanisms define a research agenda for meaningful human–AI oversight in risk analysis.

5.2. Practical Implications

The media patterns identified in this study suggest that practical governance should shift from procedural compliance toward auditable conditions for substantive oversight. The five governance themes specify different parts of this operational problem. Actionable explainability concerns whether reviewers can understand the basis and limits of AI outputs. Algorithmic auditing concerns whether bias, error, and drift are independently monitored. Cognitive friction design concerns whether workflows preserve independent human judgment. Oversight and liability concern escalation, appeal, and responsibility pathways. Institutional governance concerns the organizational structures that sustain these safeguards across cases.

Domain‐specific implementation remains important. Healthcare emphasizes diagnostic justification and review time; finance and banking emphasize auditing and fairness guardrails; justice and policing emphasize bias mitigation and safeguards against overreliance; and defense emphasizes loss‐of‐control prevention and liability assignment.

5.3. Limitations and Future Research

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the data are news reports, not direct observations of internal organizational practice. Media coverage may overrepresent failures, controversies, and dramatic cases while underreporting routine successes (Chuan et al. 2019; Fast and Horvitz 2017). Media framing can shape which failure modes become visible and how responsibility is attributed. Future research should compare media portrayals with organizational records, field studies, interviews, or survey evidence.

Second, the corpus is restricted to English‐language records from Factiva's Major News and Business Sources. Although no additional geographic restriction was imposed, the findings remain shaped by English‐language source availability, editorial selection, and Factiva's historical coverage and digitization patterns. These constraints matter especially for early years and for non‐Western governance debates. Cross‐linguistic and cross‐media‐system comparisons would clarify how human–AI teaming risks are framed outside English‐language news ecosystems.

Third, the computational pipeline introduces classification and reproducibility limitations. LLM‐assisted screening and extraction were manually validated, but errors may remain in ambiguous multi‐domain cases or complex role configurations. Open coding also depends on prompt wording, model behavior, and model‐version stability. We report prompts, validation metrics, and aggregation rules, but future studies should test prompt sensitivity, compare models, and preserve model and software versions.

Fourth, the governance mechanisms identified here reflect public discourse and elite regulatory language more than verified implementation. Cognitive friction design, algorithmic auditing, and liability assignment appear as prominent responses in reported cases, but this does not show that organizations have adopted them effectively. Future research should evaluate these mechanisms through longitudinal studies, field experiments, and operational audits that measure review quality, error correction, appeal outcomes, and human–AI disagreement management.

6. Conclusion

This study analyzes a 1956–2025 Factiva search window of 184,282 AI‐related news articles using a funnel‐shaped mixed‐methods design, with substantive findings concentrated in the 3571 screened articles after 2015. The dominant media portrayal is the AI‐predicts, human‐decides configuration, whose topic salience shifts in the post‐2022 period alongside the rise of generative AI coverage. Inductive thematic analysis identifies five pairs of mirror‐mapped failure modes and governance mechanisms linking opacity, bias, human agency, systemic fragility, and accountability to corresponding governance responses. Public discourse repeatedly portrays formal human‐in‐the‐loop requirements as insufficient unless workflows support informed and independent judgment. The central implication is therefore to design and audit the cognitive, temporal, informational, and institutional conditions under which human presence can become meaningful oversight.
