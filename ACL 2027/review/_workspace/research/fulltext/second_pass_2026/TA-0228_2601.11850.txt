Abstract
The growing integration of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) into qualitative research necessitates
closer examination of how such tools shape analytic practice, judgment, and epistemic authority. This study
investigates how researchers interact with an Inductive Thematic Analysis GPT (ITA–GPT), a purpose-built
AI tool designed to operationalize established inductive thematic analysis procedures through interactive
semi-auto prompts aligned with Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis and Naeem’s verbatim coding
principles. Guided by a Human–Artificial Intelligence Collaborative Inductive Thematic Analysis (HACITA)
framework, grounded in reflexive thematic analysis, human-in-the-loop AI, and distributed cognition, the
study focuses on analytic process rather than substantive content. Three experienced qualitative researchers
independently conducted ITA–GPT–assisted analyses of interview transcripts from education research in the
Ghanaian teacher education context. The ITA–GPT tool guided analysts through familiarization, verbatim
(in-vivo) coding, gerund-based descriptive coding, and theme development, while enforcing trace-to-text in-
tegrity, transcript coverage checks, and auditability. Data sources included interaction logs, AI-generated
tables, researcher revisions, comments, deletions, insertions, and reflexive memos. Findings show that ITA–
GPT functioned as a procedural and methodological scaffold, structuring analytic workflow and enhancing
transparency. However, researchers consistently exercised epistemic authority through five recurrent analytic
actions: modification, deletion, rejection, insertion, and commenting. These actions were essential for cor-
recting AI literalism, restoring contextual and emotional nuance, strengthening audit trails, and aligning
interpretations with institutional and professional realities. Verbatim codes were perceived as the most reliable
analytic foundation, while AI-generated abstractions required systematic human refinement. The study con-
tributes a theoretically grounded account of how inductive thematic analysis is enacted through human–AI
collaboration.
Keywords: Human–AI collaboration; Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI); Inductive thematic analysis;
Qualitative data analysis; Epistemic authority; Reflexive methodology

1. INTRODUCTION
Recent studies have increasingly explored the use of artificial intelligence (AI) to support quali-
tative data analysis, particularly through automated or semi-automated coding, text classification,
and pattern detection. Much of this work frames AI as a technical instrument (AI-assisted quali-
tative analysis) intended to improve efficiency, scalability, or consistency in qualitative
workflows, often drawing on human-in-the-loop paradigms to ensure human oversight and ethi-
cal accountability (Amershi et al., 2014; Shneiderman, 2020; Mosqueira-Rey et al., 2023). For
example, Sterczl and Csiszárik-Kocsir's (2025) works demonstrated AI-assisted qualitative anal-
ysis as a hybrid analytic process in which AI tools support coding and pattern identification,
while human researchers retain full interpretive control and responsibility for meaning-making.
A smaller body of human-computer interaction (HCI) research has begun to examine interactive
systems for AI-assisted qualitative coding, demonstrating that AI-generated codes or rules can
support early analytic exploration when reviewed and refined by human researchers (Ge-
breegziabher et al., 2023; Cook et al., 2025). In particular, Gebreegziabher et al. (2023) introduce
PaTAT, an AI-enabled qualitative coding system designed to support thematic analysis through
interactive program synthesis. Rather than treating AI as a static code generator, PaTAT learns
interpretable coding patterns in real time from researchers’ annotations, allowing users to in-
spect, revise, and merge codes as their analytic understanding evolves. However, these studies
largely evaluate AI support in terms of output quality or usability, offering limited insight into
how meaning is interpretively produced through human–AI interaction during analysis.
      In parallel, qualitative methodologists have emphasized that inductive thematic analysis is a
reflexive, iterative, and theory-informed practice in which codes and themes are actively con-
structed by researchers rather than discovered in data (Braun & Clarke, 2006, 2021, 2022). Yet,
this epistemic stance has rarely been integrated into studies of AI-supported qualitative analysis,
resulting in a conceptual disconnect between inductive qualitative theory and AI-enabled ana-
lytic practice. This study addresses this gap by reconceptualizing AI-supported coding as a
human-AI collaborative inductive analytic process in which human judgment, intervention, and
reflexive sensemaking - shaped by researchers’ experiences and positionalities- are treated as
central analytic phenomena rather than as corrective steps. Drawing on reflexive thematic analy-
sis and process coding, AI-generated codes are treated as provisional analytic prompts that
require human interpretation, revision, and contextual alignment to support rigorous meaning-
making (Saldaña, 2016; Braun & Clarke, 2021).
           The study further adopts a human-in-the-loop orientation that foregrounds epistemic au-
thority and accountability, examining how researchers assert analytic control through moments of
acceptance, modification, or rejection of AI suggestions (Shneiderman, 2020). By incorporating
distributed cognition theory, the analysis conceptualizes meaning-making as distributed across hu-
man analysts, AI systems, prompts, and analytic artifacts, allowing for a fine-grained examination
of where and how analytic reasoning unfolds during collaboration (Hutchins, 1995; Hollan et al.,
2000). In doing so, the study provides a theoretically grounded and methodologically transparent
account of human-AI collaborative inductive thematic analysis, responding directly to calls to
move beyond instrumental accounts of AI use toward deeper examinations of how AI reshapes
qualitative analytic practice. Th following research questions guided the study: How does ITA-

2. LITERATURE REVIEW
2.1.     THEMATIC ANALYSIS
Thematic analysis is a widely used qualitative research method that helps researchers identify,
analyze, and report patterns (themes) within qualitative data. It is flexible and accessible, making
it valuable across fields like psychology, education, sociology, and healthcare (Braun & Clarke
2006). The process involves familiarizing oneself with the data, generating initial codes, and
searching for themes, followed by reviewing, defining, and naming the themes. Over time, the-
matic analysis has evolved from a simple descriptive method to a more complex analytical
approach, with its flexibility allowing application across various data types. Although Braun and
Clarke’s (2006) framework are a popular guide, thematic analysis has seen ongoing development
such as those Naeem et al. (2023) who provide a novel six-step for conceptual model development
in qualitative research.

Amidst this popularity, there’s no universally agreed-upon workflow for thematic analysis, and
scholars continue to debate best practices, particularly around the distinction between inductive
and deductive approaches and the role of theory in coding. For example, in an inductive approach,
themes emerge directly from the data, whereas in a deductive approach, themes are guided by pre-
existing theories (Naeem et al., 2023). Additionally, there are disagreements about coding granu-
larity, with some researchers advocating for detailed, micro-level coding, while others prefer
broader, thematic coding (Nowell et al. 2017). These discrepancies, along with issues like varia-
bility in theme identification, subjectivity in interpretation, and lack of transparency, have
highlighted the need for more consistent, transparent, and reliable methods, areas where GenAI
can make a significant impact.

2.2.     GENAI AND THEMATIC ANALYSIS
There have been significant advancements in research exploring the potential of GenAI in both
quantitative and qualitative data analysis (Zambrano et al., 2023; Perkins & Roe, 2024). Several
studies have shown that GenAI has the potential to provide more consistent outputs than human
coders, especially when handling large datasets. This consistency stems from the AI’s ability to
avoid researcher fatigue and biases, ensuring reliable results over time (De Paoli, 2024; Acheam-
pong & Nyaaba, 2024). This section reviews key studies that examined the role of GenAI in
qualitative analysis, highlighting both its strengths and limitations. Perkins and Roe (2024) inves-
tigated the use of ChatGPT (GPT-4) for inductive thematic analysis, comparing traditional human
coding with AI-assisted analysis. Their findings indicate that while GenAI enhances data pro-
cessing and theme identification, it still requires human expertise for nuanced interpretation.
Notably, GenAI-generated themes were aligned with human-coded results, demonstrating their
potential to expedite the thematic analysis process. Similarly, other studies showed that using GPT-
3.5 Turbo API in inductive thematic analysis of semi-structured interviews can infer most human-
identified themes (De Paoli, 2024). A comparison between GenAI and traditional manual coding

2.3. CHALLENGES IN USING GENAI FOR CODING
Despite GenAI efficiency in qualitative data analysis, GenAI presents several challenges that re-
quire careful consideration. One key issue is inconsistencies and hallucinations, where GenAI
generates themes or interpretations that are not present in the original data, as noted by Perkins
and Roe (2024) and De Paoli (2024). This raises concerns about validity and the need for human
verification. Additionally, studies such as Morgan (2023) and Lee et al. (2024) highlight that
GenAI effectively identifies basic themes but struggles with subtle, interpretative nuances, making
it less reliable for capturing deeper meanings within qualitative data.
Another challenge is token processing limits, which requires researchers to split large datasets into
smaller segments, a scenario which can disrupt thematic coherence (De Paoli, 2024). Moreover,
structured prompt engineering is essential for obtaining accurate and meaningful outputs, as em-
phasized by Bijker et al. (2024). Without carefully designed stepwise prompts, GenAI may
produce vague or misaligned codes, necessitating iterative refinements. Lastly, GenAI still re-
quires human oversight, particularly for validating codes and refining themes to ensure contextual
accuracy (Lee et al., 2024; Morgan, 2023). Furthermore, while GenAI accelerates the coding pro-
cess, its dependence on human expertise for interpretation and validation underscores its role as a
complementary tool rather than a full replacement for human qualitative analysis.

3.    METHOD

The study adopted a qualitative and human-AI collaborative research method to examine how
researchers interacted with, revise, and interpret AI-generated analytic prompts during qualitative
analysis (Gebreegziabher et al., 2023; Braun & Clarke, 2021). This methodological approach is
appropriate for investigating analytic processes, judgment, and sensemaking, particularly in con-
texts where AI functions as a supportive analytic scaffold rather than an autonomous decision-
maker (Braun & Clarke, 2006, 2021; Saldaña, 2016). The design was further informed by human-
in-the-loop and distributed cognition perspectives, which emphasized interaction, reflexivity, and
the distribution of analytic work across humans and tools (Amershi et al., 2014; Hutchins, 1995;
Grinschgl & Neubauer, 2022).

3.1. STUDY CONTEXT
This study was conducted within an ongoing qualitative research program focused on education
policy and practice in Ghana, specifically within Early Childhood Education and Basic Education
contexts. The original interview transcripts addressed issues related to lecturer recruitment, spe-
cialization, professional experience, and preservice teacher preparation in Colleges of Education.
These transcripts provided a substantively rich and contextually grounded setting for examining
qualitative analysis practices that require interpretation, contextual sensitivity, and alignment with
research questions. For the present study, the substantive content of the transcripts was not the
object of analysis. Instead, the transcripts functioned as stable analytic inputs through which re-
searchers engaged in human–AI collaborative inductive analysis. The study context was therefore
used to ensure ecological validity and meaningful engagement during analysis, while the analytic

focus remained on process, specifically how researchers interacted with, revised, and reflected on
AI–generated analytic suggestions.

3.2. PARTICIPANTS
Three researchers participated in the study, each serving as an analytic participant rather than a
research subject. All participants had prior training and experience in qualitative research and
shared familiarity with the Ghanaian education context in which the transcripts were situated.
Their backgrounds included experience in education research, qualitative coding, and engagement
with issues related to teacher education and educational policy. Each researcher independently
engaged in GPT-assisted inductive coding of an assigned transcript, followed by systematic human
revision and structured reflection. Their role in the study was not to generate substantive findings
about the transcripts, but to interact with the AI tool, enact analytic judgment through revision and
re-prompting, and reflect on their analytic decisions. This design positioned participants as co-
analysts, enabling the study to examine variation and commonality in how human judgment and
sensemaking were enacted in collaboration with AI during qualitative analysis.

3.3.     DEVELOPMENT AND SYSTEM PROMPTS
The development of the AI tool followed a two-level approach. First, a stepwise prompt strategy
was adopted to test the model’s capacity to generate inductive codes and themes directly from
transcript data using Python scripts. At this stage, the scripts were used to configure the model
role, temperature, and output limits to assess whether the model could independently extract initial
codes while explicitly supporting each code with the exact phrases or statements from which it
was derived. Results from this phase demonstrated that the model could generate individual codes
and organize them into preliminary categories while maintaining traceability to the source text.
This validation informed the second phase of development.
In the second phase, the validated prompt strategies were migrated into an interactive, semi-auto-
mated system prompt to develop a customized GPT-based analytic tool. Python scripts served as
the foundational infrastructure, while the analytic workflow was aligned with inductive thematic
analysis (ITA) procedures. The resulting system was designed not as an autonomous analyst but
as a structured analytic scaffold that supported researchers in progressing through familiarization,
coding, and refinement stages.


3.3.1.   API Integration and Text Segmentation
The OpenAI API (GPT-4 Turbo) was integrated into the workflow using Python. The model tem-
perature was set to 0.3 to promote focused and deterministic outputs, and the maximum token limit
was set to 1,000 to control response length. The system role was defined as a skilled qualitative
researcher focused on inductively emerging codes, guiding the model to prioritize data-grounded
interpretations (see Figure 1). Qualitative data were uploaded as .docx files and segmented into
ten-paragraph units, each treated as a discrete analytic segment. For each segment, the API was
instructed to generate inductive codes accompanied by supporting excerpts and segment identifi-
ers. This design ensured transparency and traceability between analytic outputs and their source
data, supporting contextual interpretation and auditability. Despite the growing use of thematic
analysis, there remains no universally agreed-upon analytic workflow. Ongoing debates persist
regarding inductive versus deductive approaches, the role of theory in coding, and appropriate

3.3.2.   Customizing and Scaling the Tool
The finalized system prompt was configured as an interactive, semi-automated analytic scaffold
for inductive qualitative analysis (see Figure 2). The prompt architecture was explicitly informed
by reflexive thematic analysis principles, emphasizing iterative engagement with data, researcher
reflexivity, and the treatment of codes as provisional and revisable rather than fixed representations
of meaning. Procedural guidance from Naeem et al. (2023) informed the structured sequencing of
analytic phases, including familiarization, initial coding, and iterative refinement. The system re-
sponded dynamically to user input, requiring researchers to upload transcripts, specify research
questions, and authorize analytic progression before outputs were generated. Initial gerund-based
and in vivo codes were produced only after these human inputs, ensuring that automation remained
contingent on researcher engagement and judgment.

3.3.3.   Transcripts
The interview transcripts used in this study were drawn from a previously approved qualitative
research project that employed semi-structured interviews to explore structural and institutional
issues in the hiring of instructors and their pedagogical practices within teacher education pro-
grams in Ghana. All ethical requirements, including informed consent and institutional approval,
were met at the time of original data collection. The transcripts were collected as part of that ap-
proved study and were not generated specifically for the present research. Participants had
consented to the use of their data for research purposes, and all identifying information had been
removed prior to secondary use. For the purposes of the present study, the transcripts were repur-
posed solely as analytic material to examine human–AI collaboration during qualitative analysis.

The study did not analyze, interpret, or report findings related to the substantive content of the
transcripts. Instead, the transcripts functioned as stable input texts through which researchers en-
gaged in GPT-assisted coding, revision, and reflection. All coding outputs, revisions, and analytic
interpretations produced in this study were conducted exclusively for the current investigation and
were analytically independent from the original study’s aims, codes, or findings.

3.3.4.   Data Collection
Data were generated through a structured human–AI collaborative workflow designed to capture
analytic interaction and human judgment. Each researcher completed a full GPT-assisted inductive
coding session for an assigned transcript and then shared the complete chat history using a shared
link. These chat histories documented AI-generated provisional codes, follow-up prompts, and
researcher responses and served as the primary data source for addressing Research Question 1.
To address Research Question 2, researchers copied and pasted the generated outputs from the
GPT-assisted inductive coding sessions into a shared copy-and-paste board in a Word document
and conducted all human revisions using tracked changes. Revisions included modification, dele-
tion, rejection, insertion, and commenting on AI-generated codes. To address Research Question
3, after completing revisions, researchers submitted structured reflections via a Google Form, doc-
umenting their experiences using the tool, their reasoning behind analytic decisions, and their
perceptions of the AI’s role.


3.4.     ANALYSIS
The study is guided by a Human–AI Collaborative Inductive Thematic Analysis (HACITA) frame-
work, grounded in reflexive thematic analysis, human-in-the-loop AI, and distributed cognition
(see Table 1). Reflexive thematic analysis provides the epistemic foundation, positioning qualita-
tive analysis as an iterative, judgment-driven process in which codes are provisional, and meaning
is actively constructed by researchers rather than extracted mechanically (Braun & Clarke, 2006,
2021). From this perspective, analytic work is understood as reflexive, situated, and dependent on
the researcher's interpretation rather than algorithmic determinism. Human-in-the-loop approaches
further justify centring human oversight, revision, and accountability when working with AI sys-
tems, emphasising that AI-generated outputs must be evaluated, governed, and corrected by human
analysts (Amershi et al., 2014; Shneiderman, 2020). This orientation foregrounds epistemic re-
sponsibility, ensuring that analytic authority remains with researchers rather than being deferred
to computational systems. Distributed cognition extends the framework by conceptualising ana-
lytic sensemaking as distributed across humans, tools, and representational artefacts, such as
summaries, code tables, revision traces, and interaction logs (Hutchins, 1995; Grinschgl &
Neubauer, 2022; Sidji et al., 2025).
Within this broader framework, we specify an Analytic Action Framework that captures the con-
crete analytic actions through which epistemic authority is exercised during AI-assisted inductive
thematic analysis. These actions include modification, deletion, rejection, insertion, and comment-
ing. Modification refers to interpretive refinement of AI-generated codes or rationales to better
reflect contextual meaning. Deletion removes AI-generated content that is contextually inaccurate
or analytically misleading. Rejection marks explicit epistemic boundary-setting, where AI inter-
pretations are deemed inappropriate or invalid for the analytic purpose. Insertion reflects human-
led sensemaking through the addition of new codes, categories, or supporting evidence not

produced by the AI. Commenting documents reflexive, evaluative, and audit-oriented reasoning,
including approvals, cautions, and methodological notes.

by the HACIA framework (Braun & Clarke, 2006, 2021). The three data sources, AI-human inter-

treated as analytically meaningful. Interaction logs were examined to trace how AI-generated ger-
und and in vivo codes were introduced and how researchers responded to analytic prompts,
addressing Research Question 1 and drawing on principles of human-in-the-loop analysis (Amer-
shi et al., 2014; Shneiderman, 2020). Code modification traces were analyzed using inductive

4. FINDINGS
4.1. HOW THE ITA TOOL STRUCTURED AND MEDIATED INDUCTIVE THEMATIC ANALYSIS
Analysis of the chat histories shows that the Inductive Thematic Analysis (ITA) tool functioned
as a structured guide that scaffolded coders through the phases of qualitative analysis described by
Braun and Clarke (2006, 2019) and Naeem (2020). Figure 3 illustrates the opening interaction of
the Inductive Thematic Analysis (ITA) assistant, showing how the tool explicitly structures the
analytic process before coding begins. The assistant requests the research focus, transcript selec-
tion, and the preferred coding mode. At the same time, coders did not simply follow the tool’s
prompts. They customised, corrected, and extended the tool’s output to protect methodological
principles and add contextual and theoretical nuance. Two themes capture this dynamic: (1) the
ITA tool as a process guide that enforces principle-based coding, and (2) coder agency in custom-
isation and methodological policing.




               Figure 3: ITA–GPT initiation and procedural scaffolding of Phase 1

4.1.1.   ITA as a Process Guide for Principle-Based Coding
The ITA tool operationalised key methodological principles by structuring the workflow across
familiarisation, initial coding, and theme development. Figure 3 depicts the ITA assistant’s explicit

                                                 11
Under Review                                                                              JDEM


transition from familiarisation (Phase 1) to initial coding (Phase 2), illustrating how coder consent
is sought before proceeding. In Phase 2, it implemented Naeem’s Exact Keyword Principle by
extracting verbatim expressions from the transcript and foregrounding “trace-to-text integrity.”
Coders received multi-column tables that preserved the participants’ language exactly as spoken
and linked each keyword to a trace, a paragraph-level context, and an analytic rationale, thereby
creating a transparent audit trail (See Figure 4). On this verbatim foundation, the tool guided coders
into Phase 3, where raw keywords were converted into gerund-based descriptive codes represent-
ing actions and processes, such as “Experiencing coercive assessment,” “Investing additional
time,” and “Teaching beyond level.” It then clustered these gerunds into code families and over-
arching themes, and in several sessions differentiated between structural and personal dimensions
(for example, systemic misallocation versus individual demotivation). Coders described this pipe-
line as a mechanism that ensured adherence to Naeem’s verbatim principles and Braun and
Clarke’s thematic phases, while still leaving room for subsequent human refinement.




      Figure 4: Transition to Phase 2 and enforcement of Naeem’s Exact Keyword Principle.

4.1.2.   Coder Agency in Customisation and Methodological Policing
Although the ITA tool provided structure, coders exercised strong agency in shaping the analysis.
They enhanced auditability by requesting additional structural detail. Coder 1, for example, asked
for a fourth column with paragraph-level context and line references so that the code log would be
“fully transparent and traceable,” and the tool regenerated the tables accordingly (see Figure 5).
Coders added interpretive nuance by revising rationales and codes to reflect professional and emo-
tional realities. When the tool produced a neutral rationale focused on “time and effort,” Coder 2
instructed it to reference “emotional labor and professional strain,” and to connect “teaching be-
yond level” to “cognitive overload,” explicitly naming the psychological and cognitive burdens of
misdeployment.

4.2.   ITA–GPT ANALYTIC SCAFFOLDING: SUMMARIES, MEMOS, REFLEXIVE
    PROMPTS, AND GUIDES
      Beyond coding and theme development, the ITA–GPT tool supported four additional ana-
lytic functions: Phase 1 data summaries, analytic memos, reflexive prompts, and step-by-step
methodological guides. Together, these features formed a structural backbone that facilitated
data familiarisation, surfaced early conceptual tensions, and supported reflexive and transparent
analysis.

4.2.1.   Data Summaries and Analytic Memos
Phase 1 summaries helped coders quickly synthesise large amounts of raw transcript data. For
Coder 3, the tool produced both a single narrative summary and a segmented, paragraph-by-para-
graph summary with interpretive notes, allowing the coder to move between a holistic view of the
interview and more localised breakdowns. In Coder 1’s dataset, the summaries highlighted “emo-
tional undercurrents” such as frustration, demotivation, and disillusionment, which might have
been missed in a purely content-oriented reading. For Coder 2, the Phase 1 summary immediately
foregrounded a tension between emphasis on methodology and emphasis on pure content across
institutional settings.
Analytic memos then offered early interpretive lenses that functioned as candidate themes. For
example, memos helped Coder 1 to identify systemic misalignment, including the combination of
early childhood and primary cohorts and power dynamics around compulsory handouts. For Coder
2, memos captured contradictions between content mastery and methodological skill, and they
documented how student quality and curiosity shaped the lecturer’s professional development.
Across Phases 1 to 6, memos for Coder 3 traced a narrative progression from complaints and frus-
tration toward constructive advocacy for reform.

reflections into formal reflexive memos. Coder 1 was asked to revisit assumptions about the link

4.3.     HUMAN–AI COLLABORATIVE REFINEMENT IN QUALITATIVE CODING
The findings show that the AI tool provided a useful starting point for qualitative coding, but hu-
man researchers remained the final arbiters of meaning. Across all interactions, Coder 1,
Emmanuel, and Coder 3 treated AI-generated codes and summaries as provisional, to be audited,
refined, or replaced. Their work clustered around three analytic themes: Human epistemic author-
ity over AI output, Contextual and emotional reframing of neutral AI language, and Institutional
and professional correction of AI literalism. These themes are presented below, supported by de-
tailed examples and quantitative summaries of researcher actions.

4.3.1.   Human Epistemic Authority and Finality
Across cases, the researchers positioned AI as a scaffold rather than an autonomous analyst. They
appreciated the structure and efficiency that AI introduced, but they insisted that human judgment
must remain final, especially when contextual meaning was at stake. Coder 1 described the AI
Phase 1 summary as “an added approach to the entire coding… I love this,” indicating that she
accepted the AI structure as a helpful starting point. However, she reported that whenever the AI
“did not maintain the contextual meaning,” she deleted that code and replaced it with a human-
generated one (see Figure 6). In practice, this included removing AI phrases that she viewed as
wordy or imprecise.

4.3.2.    Contextual and Emotional Reframing of Neutral AI Language
   The second theme concerns how researchers reworked neutral AI descriptions into language
that reflected participants’ lived experiences, particularly around emotional burden and profes-
sional adaptation. Coder 2 was the most active in this regard. He treated the AI output as a skeletal
description that required enrichment with emotional and professional nuance. For example, he
revised the neutral phrase “That takes extra time and effort” to “emotional labor and professional
strain,” explicitly naming the psychological and professional costs of working in under-resourced
conditions. He rephrased “I used the knowledge and experience I had…” as “improvised teaching
practices based on lived experience… adaptive professionalism,” which reframed improvisation
as a professional competence rather than a mere survival tactic.

    At the structural level, he changed “indicates a structural issue in the education sector” to “iden-
tifies a structural bottleneck in teacher education,” which sharpened the focus on how particular
institutional arrangements limit the preparation of teachers. He also revised “The students… chal-
lenge me to prepare thoroughly” to “signals reciprocal learning,” positioning learners as co-
producers of knowledge and professional growth rather than passive recipients.

4.3.3.   Institutional and Professional Correction of AI Literalism
The third theme captures how researchers corrected AI interpretations that remained at a literal
level or misaligned with institutional realities, especially within the Ghanaian education context.
All three coders observed that the AI tended to “pick the literal meanings and not the implicit
meanings.” Coder 1 experienced this as wordiness and a lack of simplicity, Coder 2 as a lack of
emotional nuance, and Coder 3 as a failure to grasp the “broader institutional landscape.” Coder
3’s rejection of the category “Professional (dis)engagement” is one of the clearest examples. For
him, “disengagement” implied that the participant had withdrawn from professional duties (see
Figure 7). However, the participant was still working, albeit under considerable strain and misal-
location. Coder 3, therefore, deleted the category and replaced it with “Professional challenges,”
which, in his view, more accurately reflected the situation. He also added a comment explaining
that a particular code “goes beyond just mathematics” to prevent AI-driven interpretations from
narrowing systemic concerns to a single subject area.

    Coder 1 and Coder 2 also corrected literal readings, but in different ways. Coder 1 associated
some AI codes with overly technical expressions that did not match how lecturers’ behaviour
would be discussed in practice. Her substitution of “signals ethical concern” with “unprofessional”
is one example. Coder 2 countered literal and neutral phrasing by linking codes explicitly to struc-
tural and epistemic issues. For instance, his shift from “structural issue” to “structural bottleneck”
and from “epistemological disconnects” to “epistemological disjuncture” positioned the problems
not simply as generic issues, but as systemic blockages and hierarchically ordered gaps. Across
these actions, the coders did not only correct language. They repositioned AI codes within profes-
sional and institutional narratives that made sense in Ghana. In this way, AI functioned as a
generator of initial categories, while human analysts acted as critical gatekeepers who prevented
misrepresentation and oversimplification.

4.3.4.   Cross-Coder Similarities and Differences in Human–AI Collaboration

when interacting with AI. All three initially accepted the AI structure as helpful. Coder 1 described
the Phase 1 summary as an “added approach,” Coder 2 reported that AI “did an incredibly amazing
job” in reducing manual work, and Coder 3 considered the AI’s ability to associate words and
meanings as “above average.” At the same time, each researcher identified limits in the AI’s con-
textual depth, emotional nuance, and institutional awareness.
They also used their lived experience as an audit tool. Coder 1 drew on her experience as a tutor
in a low to middle-income context, Coder 2 drew on his teaching in basic and university settings,
and Coder 3 relied on what he called “technological reflexivity,” using his knowledge of institu-
tional processes and fairness to judge AI outputs. In all cases, when AI conflicts arose, human
codes and interpretations were upheld. Table 5 summarises these shared perspectives and illus-

4.4.     CODERS REFLECTIONS ON HUMAN–AI COLLABORATION
Analysis of coders’ reflections reveals four interrelated themes that characterise how human ana-
lysts experienced and negotiated collaboration with AI during inductive thematic analysis. Across
all themes, AI functioned as a procedural and organisational scaffold, while humans retained in-
terpretive authority, particularly in relation to contextual meaning, emotional nuance, and
theoretical positioning.

4.4.1.   Structural Efficiency versus Contextual Depth
All three researchers recognised AI’s strength in accelerating analytic work and structuring large
qualitative datasets. However, they consistently noted limitations in the AI’s capacity to capture
emotional nuance and implicit meaning. Fr example, Coder 1 acknowledged the organisational

                                                22
Under Review                                                                              JDEM


value of AI but emphasised the need for human depth, noting that AI “organize[s] and surface[s]
key themes,” yet often “neutralize[s] emotionally charged language.” Similarly, Coder 2 described
the AI as doing an “incredibly amazing job” in reducing manual workload and drawing meaning
from transcripts, while still recognising moments where interpretations required human refine-
ment. Coder 3 was more critical, observing that the AI’s “sense of contextual meaning is narrow
as compared to that of humans” and that it tended to “pick the literal meanings and not the implicit
meanings.”

4.4.2.   Interpretive Stance and Lived Experience as Analytic Filters
Researchers’ epistemological orientations and professional experiences strongly shaped how they
evaluated AI-generated codes. For instance, Coder 2 explicitly aligned with a constructivist stance,
describing meaning as “co-constructed between the participants and the researcher,” which led
them to accept many AI codes as representative of participant experiences. Coder 1, working from
a constructivist orientation and personal teaching experience, revised AI outputs to foreground
themes such as improvisation and professional legitimacy that were initially treated neutrally by
the AI. Coder 3 adopted a critical theorist position, actively searching for “underlying institutional
dynamics” and “emancipatory goals.” While they described being impressed when the AI acted as
a “critical analyst,” they rejected codes that failed to connect individual statements to broader
“power dynamics.”

4.4.3.   Differential Reliability of Code Types (In-Vivo vs. Gerunds)
Across coders, in-vivo (verbatim) codes were consistently viewed as the most reliable analytic
starting point. This was explicit as Coder 1 reported that in-vivo codes were preferable because
they “preserved the participant’s exact language,” whereas gerund codes often required revision
to recover “contextual meaning.” Coder 2 similarly affirmed the value of verbatim coding but
observed that some AI-generated codes “were just too long to be codes” and therefore required
simplification. Coder 3 strongly favoured “exact keyword (verbatim) coding” for capturing mean-
ingful data segments and expressed discomfort with gerund codes such as “reinforcing
dissatisfaction due to misfit,” which they felt could alternatively reflect an “unwillingness to
adapt.”

5. DISCUSSION
The findings contribute to ongoing debates in thematic analysis and AI-assisted qualitative re-
search by empirically examining how human analysts and generative AI interact during inductive
thematic analysis (De Paoli, 2024; Lee et al., 2024). This aligns with prior human–computer inter-
action research emphasizing that GenAI is useful support but still requiring human interpretation,
trust calibration, and iterative review (Wen et al., 2025; Yan et al., 2023). Building on prior work
in reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006, 2021) and recent studies on GenAI-sup-
ported qualitative coding (Perkins & Roe, 2024; Lee et al., 2024), the findings clarify how and
where epistemic authority is exercised when AI is integrated into analytic workflows. Consistent
with prior studies, the findings show that AI is most effective as a procedural and organisational
scaffold rather than as an independent analyst. Similar to Perkins and Roe (2024) and De Paoli
(2024), the ITA tool supported rapid data familiarisation, initial code generation, and theme clus-
tering. However, the present study extends this literature by demonstrating that procedural support

                                                 23
Under Review                                                                             JDEM


alone does not constitute analytic interpretation. Instead, interpretive authority remained with hu-
man analysts, who treated AI outputs as provisional and revisable. This finding aligns with Braun
and Clarke’s (2006, 2021) position that thematic analysis is an iterative, judgment-driven practice
rather than a mechanical extraction of meaning. While GenAI can enhance efficiency and con-
sistency, it does not replace the interpretive labour required to construct themes that are
theoretically meaningful and contextually grounded.
    A central contribution of this study is the identification of modification, deletion, rejection,
insertion, and commenting as systematic analytic actions through which researchers govern AI
outputs. Prior research has acknowledged the need for human verification and refinement (Mor-
gan, 2023; Lee et al., 2024) but has rarely specified how this oversight is enacted in practice. The
present findings address this gap by making epistemic labour visible through interaction logs and
revision traces. These actions operationalise what human-in-the-loop scholars have described as
oversight and accountability (Amershi et al., 2014; Shneiderman, 2020). Rather than assuming that
human judgment implicitly moderates AI outputs, this study shows that epistemic authority is ex-
ercised through observable, recurring analytic practices that protect trace-to-text integrity,
contextual accuracy, and methodological rigor.
    The finding that verbatim (in-vivo) codes were consistently viewed as the most reliable analytic
foundation aligns with Naeem et al.’s (2023) emphasis on exact keyword extraction and traceabil-
ity. While prior studies report that GenAI can infer basic themes (Morgan, 2023; De Paoli, 2024),
this study demonstrates that abstraction beyond verbatim coding often introduces interpretive risk.
Gerund-based and categorical codes frequently required human intervention to recover implicit
meanings, emotional nuance, and institutional context. This supports critiques that GenAI per-
forms well at surface-level pattern detection but struggles with deeper interpretive work (Lee et
al., 2024). The findings therefore reinforce arguments that inductive thematic analysis must prior-
itise verbatim grounding before abstraction, particularly when AI systems are involved. This
finding resonates with Nowell et al.’s (2017) concerns about subjectivity and transparency in the-
matic analysis, while also demonstrating how AI-assisted workflows can make such interpretive
moves explicit and auditable rather than implicit.
    The findings further align with Braun and Clarke’s (2021) emphasis on reflexivity by showing
that coders’ epistemological orientations and professional histories shaped how AI outputs were
evaluated. Unlike prior GenAI studies that treat researchers as interchangeable validators, this
study demonstrates that analytic sensemaking is distributed across humans, tools, and representa-
tional artefacts, consistent with distributed cognition theory (Hutchins, 1995; Grinschgl &
Neubauer, 2022). These findings support and extend existing literature by clarifying that GenAI
enhances efficiency and structure but does not resolve longstanding challenges in thematic analysis
related to interpretation, context, and epistemic judgment. Rather than offering a universal work-
flow, AI-assisted thematic analysis requires explicit frameworks, such as HACITA, that
foreground human judgment, reflexivity, and analytic accountability.

6. LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE RESEARCH
This study has several limitations. To begin with, it involved a small number of experienced qual-
itative researchers and a limited set of interview transcripts, which constrains the generalisability
of the findings to other researcher populations, disciplinary contexts, or levels of qualitative ex-
pertise. The study also focused on analytic processes rather than substantive analytic outcomes; it
did not evaluate whether AI-assisted analysis produces higher-quality themes compared to fully

                                                 24
Under Review                                                                             JDEM


human analysis. In addition, the analysis examined interaction with a single AI-assisted analytic
tool configured around inductive thematic analysis. Different models, prompting strategies, or in-
terface designs may produce different forms of human–AI collaboration. Finally, although the
transcripts were situated within a Ghanaian education context, all coders shared familiarity with
this setting, which may have shaped the types of revisions and judgments observed.
   Future research can extend this work in several ways. Studies comparing novice and expert
researchers would help clarify how analytic actions such as rejection, insertion, or reframing de-
velop with methodological expertise. Comparative research examining analytic outcomes across
human-only, AI-assisted, and hybrid workflows would complement the present process-oriented
focus. Further work is also needed to examine design features and interface affordances, such as
reflexive prompts and audit trails, that support epistemic control and transparency. Finally, extend-
ing this analytic framework to other qualitative methods and cultural contexts would help
determine the broader applicability of human–AI collaborative analytic practices.

7. CONCLUSION
The present study examined human–AI collaboration during inductive thematic analysis, shifting
attention away from AI outputs as end products and toward analytic interaction as an empirical
object of study. Grounded in reflexive thematic analysis using HACITA, human-in-the-loop AI,
and distributed cognition, the study adopted a qualitative, process-oriented design that treated in-
teraction logs, revision traces, and researcher reflections as primary data. Rather than evaluating
whether AI-generated themes were “correct,” the analysis focused on how researchers engaged
with AI suggestions, how they evaluated and revised them, and how analytic reasoning unfolded
across humans, prompts, and artifacts. The findings show that AI functioned as a procedural and
organisational scaffold, supporting familiarisation, verbatim coding, and theme clustering, but did
not operate as an autonomous analyst. Across all analytic phases, human researchers consistently
retained epistemic authority, enacting judgment through five recurring analytic actions: modifica-
tion, deletion, rejection, insertion, and commenting. These actions constituted systematic analytic
labour through which contextual meaning, emotional nuance, institutional relevance, and trace-to-
text integrity were preserved. Verbatim (in-vivo) coding emerged as the most reliable foundation
for human-AI collaboration, while gerund-based and categorical codes frequently required human
refinement to recover implicit meaning and lived experience. Researchers repeatedly corrected AI
literalism, reframed neutral language to reflect professional strain and institutional realities, and
realigned codes with contextual knowledge of Ghanaian education.
