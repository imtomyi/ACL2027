Abstract
Purpose:
As artificial intelligence (AI), such as ChatGPT, becomes integrated into scientific research, there is
increasing interest in applying AI to qualitative data analysis, a process historically dependent on labor-
intensive human interpretation. While AI’s value in quantitative analysis is established, its performance in
qualitative research necessitates further evaluation to ensure efficiency does not compromise
interpretive depth. This study compares inter-coder reliability (ICR) of a human-derived codebook (HC)
versus an AI-derived codebook (AIC) applied to the same qualitative surgical dataset to assess AI’s
performance in early-stage thematic analysis in surgical qualitative research.



Methods:
An open-ended survey identified barriers surgeons face when performing laparoscopic common bile
duct exploration compared with endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography. Two readers
performed inductive coding of 200 responses to generate the HC. The same dataset was uploaded into
ChatGPT for zero-shot inductive clustering to generate the AIC, which the same coders then applied to
the dataset. ICR was evaluated with percent agreement rate and Cohen’s kappa coefficient with
bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals (CIs) in RStudio for HC and AIC. Gwet’s AC1 value was calculated
to provide a chance-corrected agreement method.



Results
Both codebooks identified 13 codes, five of which were identical (equipment, time constraints, cost
constraints, support, and low case volume). Overall agreement rate was 95.1% for HC and 92.3% for AIC.
ICR (Cohen’s kappa) was 0.754 [95% CI: 0.711 to 0.792] for HC and 0.638 [95% CI: 0.591 to 0.684] for
AIC; Δκ (κ_AIC − κ_HC) was − 0.116 [95% CI: -0.168 to -0.059]. Qualitatively, both codebooks produced
similar overall themes, however HC tended to have more separated themes, while the AIC tended to have
more overlapping, overarching themes which may have led to coding ambiguity.



Conclusions
Both human- and AI-generated codebooks were able to generate similar overarching themes within a
shared qualitative dataset, however HC demonstrated slightly higher inter-coder agreement, within the
context of clearer thematic boundaries within the human-generated codebook. Our findings suggest that
AI can efficiently highlight overarching concepts in this qualitative surgical dataset; however, human
involvement may still be necessary to refine codebook definitions. This may indicate the feasibility of a



                                                  Page 2/18
hybrid approach, where AI performs initial clustering followed by human refinement to optimize
efficiency and interpretive accuracy in surgical qualitative research.


Introduction
Artificial intelligence (AI) systems capable of natural language processing are becoming increasingly
integrated into scientific research workflows. Large language models (LLMs), including platforms such
as ChatGPT, can proficiently summarize text, recognize patterns, and synthesize data across diverse
domains [1]. In quantitative research, AI-assisted approaches have improved efficiency in data extraction,
predictive modeling, image analysis, and large-scale data processing [2–6].

The integration of AI into qualitative research, however, has been more limited. This is due in part to
concerns regarding AI’s ability to distinguish subtle conceptual differences, interpret context-dependent
meaning, and critically reflect on how analytic decisions are made, skills inherent to human qualitative
analysis [7, 8]. These qualitative methods rely on iterative interpretation and careful code development,
making them time-intensive. As a result, there is growing interest in whether AI can assist in accelerating
qualitative workflows, particularly in reducing the time between data collection and analysis [9–13].

This is particularly pertinent in the context of thematic analysis, one of the most common approaches
for analyzing qualitative data. Thematic analysis is a structured method used to identify and interpret
recurring patterns within textual data [14]. Investigators systematically examine responses, assign codes
to meaningful segments, and organize related codes into themes that address the research question.
This process may be inductive, with themes emerging directly from the data, or deductive, through
applying predefined categories to the dataset [15]. Throughout the analytic process, code definitions are
refined to improve conceptual clarity and reduce overlap, often through discussion and reliability checks
when multiple reviewers are involved. Earlier studies have evaluated more traditional LLMs and their
ability to thematically organize qualitative data sets, demonstrating that although earlier AI models may
be effective at identifying broad thematic patterns, it may lack the ability to generate nuanced, well-
differentiated thematic structures, especially in complex datasets [13].

As LLMs continue to develop, more research is being conducted to evaluate their performance in
qualitative data analysis, particularly regarding thematic analysis. Recent studies suggest that modern
LLMs continue to reliably identify broad thematic patterns and generate initial code structures that align
with human-derived findings [10, 16]. However, important challenges with modern LLMs still exist.
Despite similar broad thematic identification, AI-generated analyses demonstrated variability in thematic
granularity, often placing more emphasis on subthemes that human researchers did not consider distinct
or thematically important to warrant separate thematic naming [10, 11]. Additionally, challenges such as
hallucinations, modification of supporting text, and prompt-dependent variability raise concerns about
the reliability and reproducibility of AI-assisted qualitative outputs [9, 11, 17]. Despite recognized benefits
of AI-assisted analysis such as improved efficiency in early coding stages through reduced manual
workload, prior research also called for improved contextual and nuanced understanding and analysis of

                                                   Page 3/18
qualitative data provided [10, 11]. Consequently, the majority of recent studies support hybrid models of
qualitative data analysis, where AI is treated as an additional researcher or tool to assist in various
stages of the qualitative research process, and human researchers continue to provide thematic
refinement and analytic validity of AI’s initial work [9, 17].

Despite this growing foundational knowledge of LLM’s role in qualitative data analysis, there continues
to be limited insight into empirical comparisons of human- and AI-generated codebooks, particularly
within clinical and surgical contexts. In surgical research, qualitative methods are particularly useful for
exploring barriers to procedural adoption, workflow challenges, and institutional constraints that are not
readily captured through quantitative methods alone [18]. With the growing availability of AI-based tools,
comparison with traditional human-derived approaches is necessary to determine whether AI-assisted
coding can achieve reliable and defensible qualitative analysis. To our knowledge, no studies to date
have evaluated modern AI’s role in assisting qualitative data analysis within the surgical field.

Our study evaluates the feasibility of using AI-assisted thematic analysis within the context of surgical
research. Specifically, we compare the inter-coder reliability of a human-derived codebook to an AI-
derived codebook that was generated using zero-shot inductive clustering in ChatGPT 5.2. By assessing
inter-coder reliability, we aimed to determine whether AI could produce thematically robust structures
suitable for deductive application by human coders. Secondary findings include thematic comparison
between the human-derived and AI-derived codebook. .


Methods
This study was a secondary methodological analysis of de-identified qualitative survey data originally
collected to evaluate barriers to performing laparoscopic common bile duct exploration (LCBDE) [19].
This survey study was approved by the Wake Forest School of Medicine Institutional Review Board.

A total of 200 open-ended survey responses to the question, “What is the biggest barrier to performing
LCBDE at your institution?” were selected a priori for analysis. Responses were ordered alphabetically by
the first word in the response text, and included sequentially to ensure systematic and reproducible
sampling without investigator bias. During inductive coding, thematic saturation, defined as the absence
of new emergent codes, was observed within the dataset [20].

Two authors (BEL, WHI) independently performed inductive thematic analysis. Initial open inductive
coding was performed using the dataset, with codes assigned to meaningful segments of text
responses. Codes were iteratively developed and refined through constant comparison and consensus
discussion. A shared codebook was developed through the iterative process, with explicit definitions
assigned to each code to enhance conceptual clarity and minimize overlap between codes. Coding
allowed multi-label assignment, meaning a single response could be assigned multiple codes if
applicable. Following codebook finalization, both investigators independently applied the finalized
human-derived codebook (HC) to all 200 responses. Coders were blinded to each other’s assignments
during this stage.
                                                  Page 4/18
The same dataset of 200 responses was input into ChatGPT (GPT-5.2; OpenAI, San Francisco, CA) in a
single batch. A zero-shot prompting strategy was used, defined as generating outputs from the model
without providing proper examples or additional prompting [21]. This methodology was selected over a
few-shot approach to evaluate the ability of a large language model to generate a thematic codebook
without task-specific training data or iterative refinement, reflecting real-world scenarios where labeled
qualitative datasets are limited and rapid exploratory analysis is desired [22].

The following prompt was applied: “Here are 200 responses to the survey question ‘What is the biggest
barrier to performing LCBDE at your institution?’. Create a thematic codebook with a theme and thematic
explanation for these responses based on response meaning. A single response may belong to multiple
themes”.

Default model parameters were used without adjustment, and no iterative prompt refinement was
performed in order to preserve the zero-shot methodology. The resulting AI-derived codebook (AIC) was
transcribed verbatim into a structured, shared spreadsheet. The same two authors independently applied
the AIC to the dataset using the same binary, multi-label coding approach to deductively analyze the
dataset. Coders were blinded to each other’s assignments during this phase as well.

A binary variable was created to denote the presence or absence of each code in a survey response for
both codebooks. The assignment of multiple codes per survey response was allowed. Overall inter-coder
reliability (ICR) was evaluated using percent agreement and Cohen’s kappa coefficient. Code-level
agreement was further evaluated using percent agreement, Cohen’s kappa coefficient, and Gwet’s AC1
[10, 23] Analysis was performed separately for the two codebooks. Agreement statistics were
interpreted using standard benchmarks [24]. All statistical analyses were performed in RStudio (version
2024.04.2; RStudio, PBC, Boston, MA, USA).

Overall ICR was assessed separately for the HC and AIC by calculating overall percent agreement and
Cohen’s kappa across all coding decisions within each codebook. Nonparametric bootstrapped 95%
confidence intervals (1,000 resamples) were calculated separately for the HC and AIC Cohen’s kappa
estimate. To compare overall ICR between the HC and AIC, the difference in Cohen’s kappa (Δκ = κ_AIC −
κ_HC) was calculated. A nonparametric bootstrap procedure with 1,000 resamples was used to estimate
the corresponding 95% confidence interval by sampling responses with replacement while preserving the
paired coder assignments and recalculating Cohen’s kappa for each codebook in every resample. The
resulting distribution of Δκ was used to derive the 95% confidence interval.

After overall analysis, code-level ICR within each codebook was further characterized by calculating
percent agreement, Cohen’s kappa, and Gwet’s AC1 for each individual code. Nonparametric
bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals with 1,000 resamples were computed for Cohen’s kappa and AC1
for each individual code in each codebook as well.

Notably, five codes were determined to be shared across both codebooks, which allowed direct
comparison between the individually analyzed ICRs. For these five shared codes, the difference in

                                                  Page 5/18
Cohen’s kappa was calculated (Δκ = κ_AIC − κ_HC) with a nonparametric bootstrapped 95% confidence
interval.

Following quantitative analysis, both investigators conducted a structured qualitative comparison on the
two codebooks. This included evaluation of thematic separation, conceptual overlap, ease of code
application, and ambiguity during coding. Observations were documented through consensus
discussion and used to contextualize quantitative findings.

Qualitative analysis of the HC and AIC demonstrated the five shared codes as previously mentioned.
Additionally, there was thematic overlap across the remaining codes, making direct comparison
impossible. While these thematic concepts were similar, coders found that single human-derived codes
often corresponded to multiple AI-derived codes, suggesting greater thematic overlap within the AI code
structure with subsequent coding ambiguity.

Coder qualitative reflections also indicated differences in the ease of use between approaches. While the
AIC provided an initial organizational framework that facilitated early coding, additional interpretation of
coding boundaries was still required given the greater thematic overlap noted. On the other hand, the HC
allowed for more refined and defined thematic boundaries through interactive development. Application
of these codes to survey responses was also thought to be slightly easier given the familiarity with the
codes themselves. Despite these differences, both approaches still retained ambiguity in coding
decisions for responses spanning multiple themes.


Discussion
This study evaluates the feasibility of using AI-assisted thematic analysis in qualitative surgical research
by providing an empirical comparison of human- and AI-generated codebooks applied to the same
dataset. Although both approaches identified similar overarching barriers to performing LCBDE, the
human-derived codebook demonstrated higher inter-coder reliability and higher thematic separation
compared to the AI-derived codebook. These findings suggest that while AI-generated themes provided
a framework for grouping open-ended surgeon survey responses, human refinement remains important
for improving conceptual clarity and coding consistency in surgical qualitative data research.

Both the HC and AIC independently identified thirteen major themes in the survey responses. Within
these thirteen codes, both codebooks independently converged on five dominant themes: equipment,
cost, time, support, and volume. Both codebooks demonstrated high percent agreement (> 90%) and
comparable ICR for these shared codes, which may be contributed to similar thematic definitions by
humans and AI. The shared codes were also relatively explicit thematic concepts compared to other
overarching codes in both codebooks, which may have contributed to similar coding performance. This
                                                   Page 11/18
level of thematic overlap by a modern LLM, without further human prompting or guidance, suggests that
ChatGPT 5.2 has the ability to identify major patterns within provided surgical qualitative data, consistent
with prior research demonstrating modern LLM’s capability in identifying major themes in non-surgical
qualitative datasets [10, 13, 16].

Despite the similarly high percent agreement between codebooks, Cohen’s kappa coefficient
demonstrated a higher overall inter-coder reliability in the HC compared to the AIC. The difference
between the two (Δκ = -0.116; 95% CI -0.168 to -0.059) echoes this finding, suggesting that coders
achieved more consistent agreement when using the human-derived codes compared to AI-generated
ones. This may be due to higher levels of thematic ambiguity and overlap within the AIC. Furthermore,
coders reported greater difficulty applying AI-generated themes due to perceived overlap between
multiple codes. Codebook structure and code names may have also contributed to thematic ambiguity
within the AIC; while the HC was more likely to rely on language used in the survey responses
themselves to create narrowly defined themes, the AIC adopted unique terminology to create broader,
more overarching themes. This difference may have contributed to the higher overall coding consistency
observed with the HC.

Importantly, this study does not aim to equate higher inter-coder reliability with superior qualitative
analysis. While the reliability metrics provide a framework for evaluating coding consistency and
thematic clarity, qualitative rigor also depends on interpretive depth, contextual understanding, and
correct representation of the participants' perspectives. These findings should be interpreted specifically
in the context of codebook usability and coding consistency rather than as a comprehensive measure of
qualitative analytic quality.

These findings also highlight an important limitation of percent agreement as a standalone
measurement for ICR, as it does not account for agreement expected by chance. Cohen’s kappa,
therefore, may offer a more nuanced empirical comparison of inter-coder reliability. Nevertheless,
interpretation of Cohen’s kappa in this context still requires caution, as high agreement may still occur
with relatively low chance-corrected agreement, masking inconsistencies in code application when
thematic boundaries overlap. Several codes demonstrated high percent agreement but comparatively
low kappa values, a pattern previously described as the “kappa paradox,” where highly prevalent or
infrequently used codes can artifactually lower Cohen’s kappa [23]. For this reason, Gwet’s AC1 was
included as an additional reliability measurement that is less sensitive to prevalence imbalance for
individual codes [10, 23]. Across both codebooks, individual codes generally maintained high Gwet’s
AC1, even among codes with lower kappa values, suggesting that some apparent disagreement may
reflect statistical limitations of kappa rather than true inconsistency alone.

Qualitative comparison of both codebooks, however, suggests that the lower overall reliability observed
in the AIC should not be solely attributed to statistical artifacts, particularly as evaluation of individual
codes supported the presence of greater thematic overlap within the AIC. Several AI-generated codes
demonstrated high percent agreement but substantially lower kappa values, two of which were shared

                                                   Page 12/18
codes with the HC. These codes most frequently represented broader conceptual themes that
overlapped with each other, such as institutional workflow and culture, personal procedural preferences,
and overlapping viewpoints on LCBDE. By incorporating multiple related constructs within a single code
definition, code application became reportedly more difficult as one survey response’s theme broadly
applied to multiple codes. Although multi-label assignment was permitted, the inclusion of multiple
related themes within a single AI-generated code definition resulted in frequent conceptual overlap
during application. This may have led to the scenario where individual responses were simultaneously
applied to multiple overlapping AI-derived codes representing closely related concepts.

For example, the AI-generated codes “Patient Flow & Access,” “Institutional Culture and Practice
Patterns,” “Personal Score of Practice/Turf,” and “Implementation & Systems Logistics” all contained
overlapping concepts of institutional workflow, particularly concerning the choice of ERCP and GI’s
involvement in patient care. Similarly, the AIC code “Clinical Judgement & Preference” related to other
AIC codes in terms of procedural preference and knowledge, institutional or personal perceptions on
safety, and other generic concerns leading to skepticism of LCBDE. Interestingly, several AI-generated
codes with high percent agreement demonstrated wide kappa confidence intervals crossing zero, which
may reflect the known kappa paradox in the setting of low prevalence and overlapping code definitions.
Notably, corresponding Gwet’s AC1 values remained high, suggesting that overall agreement was still
substantial despite the statistical test’s limitations. These findings further emphasize the importance of
interpreting kappa alongside qualitative analysis of the codebooks themselves and their applicability.

Notably, there was also some degree of thematic overlap seen in the human codebook. Similar to the
AIC, the shared codes containing Global Support and Cost Barriers were ambiguous in the HC as well.
Other thematic overlap in the HC was seen among codes related to a physician’s scope of practice and
the factors that may impact that. Overall, however, these thematic overlaps were far less frequent than in
the AIC, possibly due to the improved thematic boundaries created through iterative codebook
development. Together, these findings suggest that while AI-generated thematic analysis may capture
broad conceptual themes, it may struggle to clearly define more nuanced codes within complex
qualitative data sets, consistent with prior research [7, 10, 12, 13]. Additional human refinement may be
required to improve thematic boundaries and reduce coding ambiguity in this dataset.

An additional distinction between the codebooks involved differences in perceived thematic importance.
There appeared some disagreement in what the AI and humans considered prevalent enough in the
qualitative data to warrant thematic identification. This is best represented by the AI-generated
“Other/Unique Comments” category, which grouped unrelated, and more uncommon, survey responses
together. Beyond the “institutional success stories” which humans considered prevalent and important
enough to be considered a standalone theme, the other survey responses included in AIC’s Code 13 were
considered more as isolated observations rather than a coherent theme seen prevalently throughout the
survey responses. This discord between AI and humans in what should be considered a
subtheme/theme has been described in prior studies as well [10, 11]. This further supports a hybrid
approach to qualitative research, in which AI assists with initial thematic organization followed by human

                                                 Page 13/18
refinement to ensure conceptual significant and appropriate inclusion and descriptions of prevalent
themes in the dataset.

Interpretation of these findings should also consider differences in the codebook development process.
While the human codebook was generated through an inductive, iterative process by the two coding
authors, the AIC was generated by zero-shot processing without the opportunity for interactive
refinement. It is possible that AI thematic boundaries may be improved if additional prompting were
provided to the LLM, which has been seen to improve AI’s performance in previous works [16]. This is
possible because as more guidelines are given to AI in thematic analysis, it becomes more similar to the
iterative approach that is inherently used in human-led thematic analysis [14]. However, a zero-shot
approach was intentionally selected in this study as we wanted to evaluate the independent performance
of a modern LLM with minimal human manipulation or input. Increased prompts or guidance to AI by
humans familiar with the data would have followed more of a hybrid approach, instead of solely looking
at AI’s independent performance. As AI continues to be incorporated in qualitative data, future studies
should evaluate whether interactive prompting strategies can improve thematic precision and coding
reliability in surgical qualitative datasets. As this was a zero-shot approach, there was one prompt used
when generating the AIC. Prior research has demonstrated the variability of LLM output based on prompt
language and structure, which was outside of this study’s scope [9–11]. Future studies should evaluate
reproducibility of codebooks across alternative prompting strategies and repeated prompt generations.

An additional methodological limitation was the use of the same two coders for both the inductive
development of the HC and the deductive application of the AIC. Because the coders participated in the
iterative creation of the HC, they may have had greater familiarity with the codebook over the AIC,
potentially favoring higher agreement during HC application. The AIC was external generated and applied
without iterative refinement by the coders, which may have led to decreased familiarity and therefore
lower coding consistency between the coders. Although the same coders were intentionally maintained
to minimize variability attributable to differences in reviewer interpretation, some degree of familiarity
bias or thematic carryover between coding with the different codebooks may have occurred. Future
studies would benefit from cross over or blinded validation designs to help reduce the familiarity bias.

Another potential limitation was that only 200 responses were used for thematic coding. While this
reached thematic saturation for the human codebook, modern LLMs have been seen to perform better
with more input [1]. While this study portrays a slightly smaller sample size of responses, future studies
could evaluate how modern LLMs perform with larger data sets which may change codebook output.
Additionally, this study was limited to short survey responses, and may not accurately represent how
modern LLMs can perform with whole qualitative interview transcripts. Prior research outside of surgical
qualitative studies have shown modern LLMs may have difficulties with interview transcripts, particularly
with hallucinations, inappropriate paraphrasing of quotes, or difficulty separating the interview questions
from responses [7, 10, 17]. This study was also limited to the beginning of thematic analysis,
intentionally limited to compare AI and human’s ability to generate a usable thematic codebook. This
study did not evaluate modern LLMs performance within the full qualitative analytic process, and

                                                  Page 14/18
therefore conclusions from this data are limited to early-stage thematic coding tasks. Future research
should evaluate modern LLMs role and capability in thematic analysis with longer transcripts of surgical
qualitative interviews, as well as how modern LLMs can perform at various stages of the qualitative
research analysis process for surgical qualitative data.

Finally, this study only examined ChatGPT version 5.2 as the modern LLM. While this was an intention to
represent the feasibility of using widely available and free AI sources in qualitative data analysis, prior
research has demonstrated that there are differences in how various AI programs perform in tasks such
as thematic analysis [17]. Future studies should consider comparing the thematic analysis ability of
multiple different modern AI programs, particularly as more advanced LLMs are being developed.


Conclusion
As artificial intelligence becomes increasingly utilized in qualitative research, our study aimed to identify
some of the benefits and limitations of its application in the initial early-stage thematic coding in surgical
qualitative data sets. While both AI and human codebooks appeared able to generate similar overarching
themes within a shared dataset, reliability measures and qualitative analysis of both codebooks revealed
slightly higher thematic clarity and defined boundaries in the human codebook compared to AI, leading
to improved code application consistency. AI’s initial use in thematic grouping may be useful to identify
overarching concepts and main themes in a surgical qualitative dataset; however our data suggests that
further human iterative refinement of codebook definitions may be needed to improve coding
consistency and thematic clarity. These findings suggest the benefits of a hybrid approach to surgical
qualitative data analysis, with improved time efficiency through AI and nuanced thematic boundaries
with human refinement. Future works should be performed to further characterize developing large
language models and their role in all aspects of qualitative research within the surgical specialties.
