1. Introduction

A “Do Not Attempt Resuscitation” (DNAR) order means a process for deciding to
withhold cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR). DNAR order-making and its
implementation are complex processes [1-3]. According to the guidelines of the
European Resuscitation Council, CPR should be withheld when there is an obvious
mortal injury or irreversible death, or there is convincing evidence that CPR would not
be consistent with the patient’s values and preferences, or in their best interests [4].
    In exploring the integration of large language model (LLM) chatbots, e.g., Microsoft
Copilot®, to data analysis, this study adopts an interpretative framework. This approach
enables a nuanced examination of how healthcare professionals (HCPs), artificial

intelligence (AI), and researchers construct and negotiate meaning, trust, and authority
in critical, highly ethical medical contexts [5,6].
     In this framework (Figure 1), the first layer comprises HCPs' opinions on the
challenges and reasoning involved in DNAR order-making. These accounts are deeply
embedded in clinical experience, ethical considerations, and patient interactions.
Understanding these descriptions is crucial, as they offer insights into the human factors
that shape decision-making [7].
     The second layer involves the data patterns identified by an LLM functioning as a
"copilot" [8]. Unlike traditional decision-support tools, Copilot® aims to augment human
judgment by offering suggestions without replacing the clinician's role. However, the
interpretability and trustworthiness of these AI-generated suggestions are crucial, as
clinicians must understand and trust the AI's reasoning to effectively integrate its
suggestions into their practice [9].
     The third layer comprises the researchers' analytical interpretations of both HCPs'
descriptions and Copilot’s® suggestions. This layer involves qualitative analysis, often
using methods such as thematic coding or discourse analysis, to identify underlying
themes, assumptions, and values in narratives and Copilot’s® outputs. Researchers
critically examine how both human and AI interpretations are constructed, highlighting
areas of alignment and divergence, and exploring the implications for clinical practice
and ethical standards [6,10].
     The fourth and final layer is a reflexive comparative synthesis in which researchers
analyze how the act of comparing human and AI interpretations constructs knowledge
and authority. It considers how epistemic authority is distributed among clinicians,
Copilot®, and researchers, and how this distribution impacts trust, decision-making (DM),
and potential biases in clinical settings [6,8-10].

     This case study aims to explore the integration of the Microsoft Copilot® LLM
chatbot into ethically sensitive clinical DM to identify the problems HCPs face in DNAR
order-making and key areas for development. Because the primary objective is to
compare human-generated and AI-assisted thematic interpretations, rather than to
develop a comprehensive model of DNAR decision-making, the methodological choices
prioritized analytic depth, consistency, and focused comparison.


2. Methods

An invitation for voluntary participation in the survey was sent via email to all doctors
and nurses working in the Tampere University Hospital (TAUH) special responsibility
area, which includes two additional central hospital districts. The survey data collection
has been reported in previous studies [1,2]. There were 893 responses to the question,

‘Are there any problems with DNAR order making?’ Of these, 524 participants
responded ‘yes’ and described their opinions. A sample of 100 healthcare professionals’
narratives was analyzed across four layers: HCPs' opinions, LLM/Copilot® thematic
analysis, researchers’ interpretations of both human and AI findings, and finally, a
synthesis comparing all results. The same two researchers (KS, EK) conducted the
interpretive and comparative phases.
     While the sample is relatively small, this size aligns with previous methodological
literature recommending focused samples for exploratory analysis of interpretive
processes [10]. Thus, this study adopted an exploratory qualitative design to examine
how a large language model (LLM) functions as an interpretive partner in an ethically
sensitive clinical context.


3. Results

Using qualitative content analysis of the question “What kinds of problems are associated
with DNAR orders?”, Copilot® identified 15 thematic categories in responses describing
issues related to DNAR protocols and DM.
     Timing of DNAR orders (1), Documentation and Communication (2), Conceptual
Clarity and Training Needs (3), Role of Patients and Families (4), Specialty-Specific
Differences (5), Palliative and End-of-Life Care (6), Ethical and Human Considerations
(7), Practical Examples and Experiences (8), Decision-Maker's Role (9), Staff
Perspectives (10), Patient’s Advance Directives (11), Need for National Guidelines(12),
Scope of Medical Interventions (13), Reversing DNAR Orders (14) and Communication
and Dialogue (15). The 15 thematic categories were referenced 149 times across the
dataset.
     Two researchers (KS, EK) synthesized analytical interpretations from both the HCPs’
descriptions and the AI data pattern a total of 115 times. A frequency scale was
developed based on the distribution of category references in the dataset. It enabled
clearer data organization and facilitated comparison between AI-generated suggestions
and researchers’ interpretations. The frequency of the opinions in categories ranged from
4 to 16 (copilot) and from 2 to 16 (researchers) (Table 1).

4. Discussion

     The interpretive framework used provided valuable insights into this ethically
sensitive clinical decision-making. The study was intentionally exploratory and did not

policy. Copilot® was employed not as an autonomous decision-maker but as a
collaborative epistemic partner, designed to augment human judgment while ensuring
that responsibility and accountability remained firmly with the human actors [6,8-10].
Using a single consistent question allowed us to control variation and focus on
differences between human and AI-assisted thematic reasoning. The 15 AI categories
summarized from 100 health care professional opinions in this case study aligned with
topics identified in previous studies [5]. However, the subjective nature of this qualitative
case study can jeopardize the reliability and validity of the results. Nevertheless, the use
of Copilot® in data analysis was transparent and interpretable, enabling researchers to
assess its relevance and reliability [6-8,10].
     The most substantial differences emerged in categories reflecting variation in
interpretive framing or analytical emphasis between AI-driven and manual analyses. The
differences in the Copilot’s® interpretations were especially evident in categories
describing the roles of patients and families as well as practical issues related to DNAR
orders. In contrast, the researchers focused on the importance of communication and
documentation. This may be due to AI finding narrative opinions difficult to interpret
and to negotiate meaning, trust, and authority in critical, highly ethical medical contexts
[6,10].
     According to the low-frequency scale, the researchers reported nine categories, with
three in each of the medium- and high-level categories. However, the four lowest
frequencies were the same in both the AI suggestion and the researchers’ views.
Regarding the number of categories, it would be necessary to test the AI on larger
datasets to improve the category content. It would also have been interesting to continue
the analysis using the categories to summarize the problems with DNAR orders and to

conduct a more in-depth analysis with a larger dataset. Future research would improve
the generalizability of the findings and enhance their practical relevance and applicability
within clinical education.
     As a conclusion, this study examined how large language models, including tools
such as Copilot®, function as interpretive partners in DNAR-related reasoning and
communication. The findings show that these models can structure clinical
communication, surface ethical considerations, and support reflective engagement, while
also reproducing limitations and assumptions present in clinical discourse. The results
suggest that LLMs like Copilot® are most useful for communication, training, and
deliberation rather than for automating clinical decisions, though further evaluation with
larger, more diverse datasets is needed to improve reliability and generalizability.
Overall, the study provides a foundation for future research on the responsible integration
of LLMs into clinical decision support and educational tools.
